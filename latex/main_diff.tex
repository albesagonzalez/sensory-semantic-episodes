\begin{abstract}
\noindent Current theories of systems consolidation primarily view replay as a one-way teaching signal from the medial temporal lobe (MTL, involved in episodic memory) to neocortex (CTX, involved in semantic memory). However, the mechanism by which semantic representations emerge from episodic experience is not yet fully understood, and even less clear is the origin or role of semantic information within episodic memory. We propose that replay establishes a shared semantic vocabulary between MTL and CTX through two complementary processes. First, episodic replay enables neocortex to extract reusable semantic concepts from overlapping episodes via  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{a form of }\DIFaddend dictionary learning. Second, spontaneous cortical activity replays these learned concepts back to MTL, allowing semantic representations to become part of the episodic code. We implement this framework in a circuit model using local Hebbian plasticity and homeostatic competition. The model reproduces key experimental observations, including concept-cell-like representations, increased overlap between related memories following consolidation, and synaptic reorganization associated with systems consolidation. Functionally, embedding semantic representations within episodic memory improves recall by providing an error-correcting scaffold and enhancing replay consistency,  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{inducing a generalization bias in the consolidation of compositional representations}\DIFaddend . More broadly, our results suggest a biologically plausible mechanism for cooperative learning across episodic and semantic memory circuits.
\end{abstract}
\section*{Introduction}
The distinction between episodic and semantic memory has been central to cognitive neuroscience since its first formalization \shortcite{tulving_1972}. In its original view, episodic memory captures individual experiences, while semantic memory accumulates generalized knowledge that emerges from repeated exposure to similar episodes \shortcite{squire_2004}. One influential computational explanation for this division is provided by the Complementary Learning Systems (CLS) framework. This theory posits that a fast-learning system (the hippocampus and the rest of the medial temporal lobe, MTL) specializes in rapidly encoding episodic memories. In contrast, a slow-learning neocortical (CTX) system works in concert with the MTL to consolidate and generalize memories \shortcite{mcclelland_1995, oreilly_2014}.
\newline\newline
One of the central assumptions of consolidation theories is that replay events originating in the hippocampus provide a teaching signal to neocortex. This idea has been remarkably successful in explaining how semantic knowledge can gradually emerge from episodic experience and remains a cornerstone of Complementary Learning Systems theory. However, the precise computational mechanism underlying replay-driven learning remains unclear. On the one hand, several influential models are strongly inspired by the machine learning paradigm \shortcite{saxe_2019, singh_2022, sun_2023, spens_2024}. As a result, they often rely on a predefined distinction between input, hidden, and output representations as the backbone of cortical learning. How such distinctions could be stored within replay events and translated into appropriate plasticity signals remains unknown. Furthermore, these approaches typically assume that hippocampal replay faithfully reinstates  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the entirety of episodic neural activity during encoding}\DIFaddend , an assumption that is difficult to reconcile with evidence that replay is sparse, reconstructive, and only partially reinstates previous activity \shortcite{swanson_2020, stoianov_2022}. Finally, learning commonly relies on error-driven or backpropagation-like mechanisms, whose biological implementation remains debated \shortcite{lillicrap_2020}. On the other hand, more biologically grounded models \shortcite{tome_2022, chrysanthidis_2022, guerreiro_2024} often assume a pre-existing shared code between hippocampus and cortex. Consequently, these models primarily address how associations between representations can be transferred from hippocampus to cortex, rather than how semantic representations themselves emerge from episodic experience. In addition, the complexity of the memories they can accommodate is limited, requiring highly decorrelated episodic patterns for stable learning and recall. Together, these considerations suggest the need to reevaluate replay as a pure re-instantiation of cortical targets for error-driven learning and to move towards a biologically plausible account that can still uncover semantics from structured, correlated episodes.
\newline\newline
A second unresolved tension between theory and experiments concerns the nature of the episodic code itself. Classical theories of associative memory have long emphasized that storage capacity is maximized when the stored patterns are decorrelated and approximately orthogonal to one another \shortcite{hopfield_1982, tsodyks_1988, treves_1991}. In contrast, a growing body of experimental evidence suggests that representations in the medial temporal lobe, a circuit crucial for episodic memory, contain substantial semantic and relational structure. These include concept cells that respond selectively to abstract concepts across different sensory modalities and contexts \shortcite{quiroga_2005, quiroga_2012, courellis_2024}, as well as neurons that encode latent variables and higher-order task structure \shortcite{aronov_2017, sun_2020}. Such representations necessarily introduce structured overlap between memories, apparently violating the \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{pattern separation }\DIFaddend conditions under which episodic memory is expected to operate most efficiently. Moreover, semantically meaningful material is paradoxically often remembered more accurately than arbitrary information  \DIFaddbegin \shortcite{chase_1973, murray_1976, tse_2007, lin_2021}\DIFaddend . This raises two fundamental questions: what is the origin of these semantic representations in the episodic memory circuit, and what computational role do they serve?
\newline\newline
All in all, while views challenging a strict dichotomy between semantic and episodic memory are almost as old as the semantic-episodic distinction itself \shortcite{mckoon_1986, greenberg_2010, gentry_2024}, we still lack a mechanistic understanding of how or why these two memory systems interact. Nevertheless, several recent developments point towards a different perspective on episodic-semantic interactions. For example, replay is increasingly viewed as a generative process, rather than providing a verbatim teaching signal for cortical learning \shortcite{swanson_2020, diekmann_2023, kurth_2023}. Under this perspective, neocortex may act less as a \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{passive }\DIFaddend recipient of replayed memories and more as an active interpreter of replayed structure, extracting a set of reusable representations that efficiently explain recurring replay events. Such a process bears a strong conceptual resemblance to dictionary learning frameworks, in which complex activity patterns are decomposed into sparse combinations of reusable latent features \shortcite{olshausen_1996, bricken_2023}. A complementary set of observations concerns the flow of information in the opposite direction. While consolidation theories have traditionally emphasized hippocampus-to-cortex transfer \shortcite{moscovitch_2021}, cortex-to-hippocampus teaching has recently been highlighted as an important and largely unexplored aspect of memory consolidation \shortcite{liu_2024}. Related ideas have begun to emerge in computational accounts that assign an increasingly active role to cortical semantic knowledge during replay \shortcite{van_2020} and episodic encoding \shortcite{spens_2024_b}. Furthermore, experimental work has revealed mechanisms through which cortical activity can shape medial temporal lobe representations, including cortex-initiated hippocampal replay \shortcite{rothschild_2017}, strengthening of corticofugal projections from cortical assemblies to medial temporal circuits \shortcite{lee_2023}, and prefrontal signals that control the overlap and linking of episodic memories \shortcite{de_sousa_2026}.  Together, these observations suggest a framework in which replay is used not simply to transfer associations from episodic to semantic memory, but to discover reusable semantic structure from episodic experience and subsequently use that structure to shape future episodic representations.
\newline\newline
Here, we present a model of episodic-semantic memory interactions that addresses the challenges outlined above. First, we put forward dictionary learning as the primary algorithm of semantic consolidation. Under this view, the role of \textit{episodic replay} is not \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{only }\DIFaddend to provide targets for cortical learning \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(}\textit{\DIFadd{what should one predict in future episodes based on those stored}}\DIFadd{)}\DIFaddend , but to generate candidate  recurring atomic representations  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{for future episodic encoding (}\textit{\DIFadd{how should one interpret future episodes that share structure with those stored}}\DIFadd{)}\DIFaddend . Second, we propose that these learned cortical representations can be transferred back into the episodic memory system. This allows semantic representations to become part of the episodic code, thus enabling a shared vocabulary in the representations used by the two memory systems. We show that these principles can be instantiated in a circuit model based on Hebbian and homeostatic local plasticity, with neocortex as the primary substrate for semantic memory and the medial temporal lobe as the primary substrate for episodic memory. We further demonstrate that the resulting dynamics reproduce key experimental observations, including concept-cell-like responses, increasing overlap between semantically related memories, and recent findings in synaptic-specific changes associated with consolidation. We then investigate the computational role of semantic representations in episodic memory and find that these act as an error-correcting scaffold that benefits recall. Furthermore, semantic representations in the medial temporal circuit enhance  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{neocrotex-MTL }\DIFaddend communication during replay, further supporting semantic consolidation of episodes that are semantically  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{in-distribution. These }\DIFaddend results provide a \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{computational framework that can explain, unlike classic accounts of systems consolidation, why the episodic memory circuit can benefit from semantic representations. They also make specific predictions that could be tested in a statistical learning paradigm where input is compositional. All in all, we provide a }\DIFaddend mechanistic account of how semantic knowledge can emerge from complex episodic experience under biological principles, in turn shaping the organization and consolidation of future memories.

\section*{Results}
\subsection*{A model for semantic structure in episodic memory based on dictionary learning}
We introduce a model of semantic and episodic memory (Fig. \ref{fig:diagram}a). The model starts from a simple psychological observation: episodic memories often contain both sensory detail and semantic content. For example,  consider multiple episodes of someone having their favourite food: \textit{pizza}. These may differ in appearance, smell, taste, and context, but still share semantic structure such as the presence of \textit{pizza}, \textit{cheese}, and \textit{tomato}. Our model captures this by positing that episodic traces contain two components (Fig. \ref{fig:diagram}a): a sensory component, preserving event-specific perceptual detail, and a semantic component, representing reusable concepts shared across events.
\newline\newline
At the circuit level, however, the presence of semantic representations in episodic memory raises a computational problem.  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Although the }\DIFaddend medial temporal lobe (MTL),  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{which is }\DIFaddend crucial for episodic memory, has been shown to contain concept-like information \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, }\DIFaddend classical attractor models  achieve maximal storage capacity when \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{stored }\DIFaddend patterns are maximally distinct \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{. Semantic representations, by contrast, }\DIFaddend introduce structured overlap between episodes. At the same time, behavioural evidence  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{points }\DIFaddend in the opposite direction \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{: semantically comprehensible episodes }\DIFaddend are better recalled, and lesions in MTL regions  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{such as }\DIFaddend the hippocampus also impair  recall of already consolidated \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{semantic }\DIFaddend information. How  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{can }\DIFaddend the episodic memory system  not only tolerate this structured overlap but actually  benefit from it? To address this question, we  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{develop }\DIFaddend a complementary circuit implementation of the model to study how semantic information can coexist with episodic storage in MTL. Following standard mappings,  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{semantic memory is assigned }\DIFaddend to neocortex (CTX) and episodic memory to MTL (Fig.  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\ref{fig:circuit_implementation}}\DIFaddend ). MTL is divided into MTL-sensory, driven by sensory input, and MTL-semantic, driven by semantic cortical input. These populations are recurrently connected, allowing sensory and semantic representations to be bound into individual episodic traces. All regions are modeled as binary networks with homeostasis (see Technical Box \ref{box:hbn} and Methods) because of their analytical tractability and direct connection to semantic learning \DIFaddbegin \shortcite{albesagonzalez_2025}\DIFaddend .
\newline\newline
A related question that can be asked with the circuit model is how the cortical semantic representations are formed in the first place. A common framing is that replayed cortical reactivations are used as a teaching signal, which can then be  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{backpropagated}\DIFaddend . However, this often leaves implicit how the reactivated episodic pattern interacts with cortical activity. Which cortical neurons provide the input? Which ones define the target? Which ones remain plastic? Even biologically motivated alternatives to backpropagation, such as Contrastive Hebbian Learning or Predictive Coding, still require a structured distinction between input, hidden, and output components during learning.
\newline\newline
Here, we avoid this problem by proposing replay as a mechanism for dictionary learning (Technical Box \ref{box:replay}). Under this view, the role of \textit{episodic replay} is to provide episodic patterns that CTX must interpret, which is achieved via Hebbian plasticity and competitive learning. This avoids backpropagation-like credit assignment and the need to predefine cortical targets. Furthermore, the replayed pattern is not a verbatim copy of awake experience, but a generalized episodic pattern. When projected to CTX, this pattern either refines existing representations or recruits new neurons if no previous representation is sufficiently close.
\newline\newline
The same mechanism can also explain how CTX transfers the learned representations to MTL. If recurrent MTL dynamics expose episodic structure to CTX, recurrent CTX dynamics can expose semantic structure to MTL. We call this process \textit{semantic replay} because, like episodic replay, it is internally generated, begins from random activity, and recovers attractor states through recurrent dynamics. However, because these attractors are shaped by CTX recurrent connections, they reflect semantic rather than episodic structure.  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{By projecting }\DIFaddend them to MTL-semantic \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, }\DIFaddend concept-like representations can enter the episodic circuit.
\newline\newline
Together, episodic and semantic replay implement in the circuit the learning dynamics proposed by the model ( \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Figs}\DIFaddend . \ref{fig:diagram}b  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{and \ref{fig:circuit_implementation}}\DIFaddend ). Episodic replay supports \textit{episodic-to-semantic consolidation}, allowing CTX to extract a cortical dictionary from episodic structure. Semantic replay supports \textit{semantic-to-episodic consolidation}, allowing MTL-semantic to incorporate the learned cortical vocabulary. We next validate this learning algorithm in simulations of the circuit model. We then use the model to ask how semantic overlap can interact beneficially with episodic attractor dynamics, supporting memory and further consolidation\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, and connect our results with previous experimental findings}\DIFaddend .

\begin{technicalbox}[box:hbn]{Homeostatic Binary Networks}
All regions in the circuit model are implemented as Homeostatic Binary Networks, following \shortciteA{albesagonzalez_2025}. These networks are binary, with sparsity in each region being controlled by a \textit{K-winners-take-all} mechanism, implemented with a $top$-$K$ activation function. For further details and definitions of variables, see Methods.
\newline\newline
\textbf{Neural dynamics.} Each region is described by a binary activity vector
\[
X^\mathrm{region}\in\{0,1\}^{N_\mathrm{region}},
\]
with exactly $K^\mathrm{region}$ active neurons at each timestep. Synaptic input is first integrated into a pre-activation variable
\[
\hat{X}^\mathrm{region}
=
W^{\mathrm{region}\leftarrow\mathrm{pre}}X^\mathrm{pre},
\]
which is then mapped into binary activity by competition:
\[
X^\mathrm{region}
=
\operatorname{top\text{-}K^\mathrm{region}}
\big(\hat{X}^\mathrm{region}\big).
\]
This mechanism enforces competition across neurons to represent the current \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{synaptic }\DIFaddend input.
\newline\newline
\textbf{Local learning.} Synapses are updated by a Hebbian rule,
\[
\Delta W_{ij}^{\mathrm{post}\leftarrow\mathrm{pre}}
=
\lambda_{ij}X_i^\mathrm{post}X_j^\mathrm{pre},
\]
followed by divisive normalization (homeostasis), which prevents runaway growth and keeps the total synaptic strength bounded.
\newline\newline
\textbf{Homeostatic regimes.} We use two forms of synaptic homeostasis. With \textit{incoming homeostasis}, weights are normalized so that the total input onto each postsynaptic neuron is bounded. With \textit{outgoing homeostasis}, weights are normalized so that the total output from each presynaptic neuron is bounded. Under these two regimes, synaptic weights converge to scaled conditional probabilities:
 \DIFaddbegin \[
\DIFadd{W_{ij}^{\mathrm{post}\leftarrow\mathrm{pre},\,\mathrm{incoming}}(\infty)
\equiv
\frac{W_{\mathrm{max\text{-}in}}^{\mathrm{post}\leftarrow\mathrm{pre}}}{K^\mathrm{pre}}
\,p(X_j^\mathrm{pre}=1\mid X_i^\mathrm{post}=1),
}\]\DIFaddend 
 \DIFaddbegin \[
\DIFadd{W_{ij}^{\mathrm{post}\leftarrow\mathrm{pre},\,\mathrm{outgoing}}(\infty)
\equiv
\frac{W_{\mathrm{max\text{-}out}}^{\mathrm{post}\leftarrow\mathrm{pre}}}{K^\mathrm{post}}
\,p(X_i^\mathrm{post}=1\mid X_j^\mathrm{pre}=1).
}\]\DIFaddend 
Thus, incoming homeostasis makes synapses approximate the average presynaptic pattern associated with a postsynaptic assembly, whereas outgoing homeostasis makes synapses reflect how strongly presynaptic neurons predict postsynaptic recruitment.
\newline\newline
\textbf{Functional role.} In recurrent connectivity, these dynamics implement pattern completion. In feedforward connectivity, they implement competitive dictionary learning, causing neurons to become selective to stable components of their input.
\end{technicalbox}
\begin{technicalbox}[box:replay]{Replay for Dictionary Learning}
Instead of viewing replay as perfect reinstatement of stored episodes, we propose it can be seen as a mechanism for dictionary learning, where a \textit{post} region learns relevant \textit{atoms} describing a \textit{pre} region based on presynaptic recurrent connections. The mechanism is as follows:
\newline\newline
\textbf{Generate a presynaptic pattern} from noise via attractor dynamics:
\[
X^\mathrm{pre}_{0} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I_{N_\mathrm{pre}})
\quad \longmapsto \quad
X^\mathrm{pre}_{n+1}
=
\operatorname{top\text{-}K_R^{\mathrm{replay}}}
\left(
W^{\mathrm{pre}\leftarrow \mathrm{pre}} X^\mathrm{pre}_{n}
\right).
\]
\textbf{Project to postsynaptic layer}
\[
X^\mathrm{post}
=
\operatorname{top\text{-}K^\textrm{post}}
\left(
W^{\mathrm{post}\leftarrow\mathrm{pre}}X^\mathrm{pre}
+
b_\mathrm{IM}^\textrm{post}\mathrm{IM^\textrm{post}}
\right),
\]
\textbf{Update dictionary} For postsynaptic active neuron $i$ ($X^\mathrm{post}_i$ = 1).
\begin{itemize}
    \item If neuron $i$ is immature ($\textrm{IM}^\textrm{post}_i$ = 1): learn new atom
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Create a one-shot receptive field $W_{ij}^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}} \propto X^\textrm{pre}_j$ \\  
        $\longrightarrow$ neuron $i$ becomes maximally selective to  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$X^\textrm{pre}$
        }\DIFaddend \item Make neuron $i$ mature ($\textrm{IM}^\textrm{post}_i$ = 0)
    \end{itemize}
    \item If neuron $i$ is mature  ($\textrm{IM}^\textrm{post}_i$ = 0): update existing atom
    \begin{itemize}
        \item  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The receptive }\DIFaddend field is pushed in the direction of $X^\textrm{pre}$
        \[W_{ij}^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}} \longmapsto
p(X^\mathrm{pre}_j=1 \mid X^\mathrm{post}_i=1)\]
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{technicalbox}
 \subsection*{A  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{neocortex-medial temporal lobe }\DIFaddend model can form semantic memories via dictionary learning}
\subsubsection*{Neocortex forms a dictionary of reusable components via episodic replay}
We first consider episodic-to-semantic consolidation (Fig. 2a) as a form of dictionary learning carried out by neocortex. In dictionary learning, complex inputs are represented by combining a small number of reusable elements (typically called \textit{atoms}, Technical Box \ref{box:replay}). Here, we refer to these learned elements as \textit{cortical words}: reusable cortical representations that capture recurring parts of episodic experience. In this view, episodic replay in MTL does not simply reinstate full episodes. Rather, sparse replay reveals recurring subpatterns embedded across many episodes. CTX then either matches each replayed candidate to a previously learned cortical word, refining the corresponding receptive field, or treats it as sufficiently novel to recruit immature cortical neurons and establish a new receptive field. In this way, repeated episodic replay gradually builds a cortical dictionary of reusable semantic components (words).
\newline\newline
We first test whether CTX can implement such an algorithm in the model. To do so, we generate episodes by  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{compositionally combining latent factors $A$ and $B$, }\DIFaddend $(A_i,B_j)$, with $A_i \in \{A_1,\dots,A_5\}$ and $B_j \in \{B_1,\dots,B_5\}$ (Figs. \ref{fig:phase_a}b and \ref{fig:input_generation}, see Methods). Each latent factor \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{can take different values }\DIFaddend (e.g. $A_4$, or $B_4$)\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, each of which }\DIFaddend corresponds to a concept  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{-a }\DIFaddend semantically coherent set of sensory  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{features- }\DIFaddend such as \textit{cheese} or \textit{tomato} (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_a}b). The distribution over pairings is structured: matched pairs, such as $(A_3,B_3)$, account for 50\% of episodes, whereas all mismatched pairings jointly account for the remaining 50\%. Thus, although every $A_i$ can in principle appear with every $B_j$,  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{factor values }\DIFaddend with matching indices co-occur more often. For example, $A_4$ is more predictive of $B_4$ than of $B_3$.
\newline\newline
During wake, episodic inputs are stored in MTL through Hebbian plasticity. During sleep, sparse pattern completion (Technical Box \ref{box:replay}) in MTL recovers sub-patterns shared across episodes (e.g. most active neurons correspond to $B_2$), rather than reinstating complete episodic states (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_a}c). These replayed subpatterns are projected to CTX through initially random CTX$\leftarrow$MTL connections (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_a}d,  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$t=0$}\DIFaddend ). If the replayed pattern is not sufficiently close to existing cortical receptive fields (i.e. no CTX neuron is strongly tuned to the current MTL pattern), cortical activity is biased toward immature neurons (Methods). Because these neurons are more excitable, they are preferentially recruited, forming a one-shot cortical representation of the replayed MTL pattern (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_a}d, \textit{word proposal}, day 1). However, when the replayed pattern is sufficiently close to an existing cortical receptive field, the corresponding cortical word is reactivated and refined (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_a}d, \textit{word refinement}, from day 1 on). This process slowly tunes cortical neurons to stable, de-noised components of episodic experience ( \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Fig}\DIFaddend . \ref{fig:phase_a}d (day 900) and \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Fig. }\DIFaddend \ref{fig:phase_a}e). \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{It should be noted that, throughout the study, the only population with a predefined (partial) tuning is MTL-sensory: other labels (e.g. $A_3$ in CTX) come from examining the selectivity of neurons after learning, and not from a pre-specified encoding (see }\textit{\DIFadd{Neuronal Ordering}} \DIFadd{in Methods).
}\DIFaddend \newline\newline
We further confirm the representational change in CTX by measuring the mutual information between cortical activity and the underlying \textit{simple concepts}, $A_i$ and $B_j$. Cortical activity rapidly becomes maximally informative about  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{latent factor values}\DIFaddend , reaching a normalized mutual information of approximately 1 (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_a}f). \textit{Complex concepts} of the form $A_iB_j$ are considered in later sections. We also find that the probability of learning a cortical word increases monotonically with the probability that its corresponding simple concept appears in an episode (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_a}g). Thus, this mechanism can be interpreted as a form of prioritizing replay for  generalization: frequently recurring compositional elements are more likely to be consolidated into cortical words (semantic representations).
\subsubsection*{Cortical recurrent connectivity represents  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{semantics as predictability across }\DIFaddend cortical words}
In our model (Fig. \ref{fig:diagram}a), we proposed that semantic memory contains information about the relatedness between concepts, which is defined based on their directional predictiveness (e.g. pizza is highly predictive of cheese). In the circuit implementation, when multiple cortical neurons become tuned to the same replayed pattern, they also become strongly recurrently connected, forming a cortical assembly for that word (Methods). This corresponds to the standard replay-mediated formation of recurrent cortical assemblies, in which reactivated neurons are bound into a stable attractor-like representation. However, during wake, slow plasticity further updates recurrent connections across the broader cortical population. Under Hebbian plasticity with outgoing homeostasis, these recurrent weights converge to the conditional firing structure between neurons \cite{albesagonzalez_2025}. Because cortical neurons are tuned to individual concepts, recurrent connectivity comes to reflect how predictive concepts are of one another (Figs. \ref{fig:phase_a}h and \ref{fig:circuit}b). Thus, while feedforward CTX$\leftarrow$MTL weights learn the dictionary entries---what each cortical word encodes--- recurrent CTX$\leftarrow$CTX connections learn the relations between entries---how each word is situated within the broader semantic structure. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Importantly, this allows storing asymmetric semantic relationships (for example, pizza is much more predictive of cheese than }\textit{\DIFadd{vice versa}}\DIFadd{), as we will see in the following sections.
}\DIFaddend \newline\newline
Together, these results suggest that semantic memory can emerge from episodic replay through cortical dictionary learning. Replay extracts candidate words from overlapping episodes, CTX stabilizes them as reusable semantic units, and recurrent cortical connectivity organizes them according to their co-occurrence statistics. In the pizza example, cortical words encode ingredients such as cheese or tomato, whereas corticocortical connections encode the predictive relationships between ingredients.

\subsection*{Semantic replay can transfer the learned vocabulary into episodic memory}
We next ask whether spontaneous cortical activity can transfer the learned cortical dictionary back into episodic memory (semantic-to-episodic consolidation; Fig. \ref{fig:phase_b}a, left). We refer to this mechanism as \textit{semantic replay} because it parallels episodic replay: it is driven by spontaneous activity, with activity patterns recovered through attractor dynamics over initial random states. However, here the replayed content comes from recurrent connections representing \textit{semantic} information, as opposed to episodic. In the pizza example, once ingredients such as tomato and cheese have been consolidated as cortical words, semantic replay can imprint these words onto MTL-semantic. Thus, before semantic-to-episodic consolidation, an episode containing tomato sauce may be represented only through sensory features. After consolidation, MTL-semantic additionally contains neurons that explicitly signal the presence of tomato, analogous to MTL concept cells. At the circuit level, this corresponds to MTL-semantic neurons developing receptive fields that are selective for cortical assemblies representing individual concepts (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_b}a, right).
\newline\newline
To support this process, we now equip the simulated model with semantic replay (Algorithm \ref{alg:sleep}).  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{After the first 1000 days of training, the network learns semantic representations in CTX, but MTL-semantic activity depends on random initial connectivity (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_b}b). After that, during sleep, semantic }\DIFaddend replay initializes CTX from random activity and allows recurrent cortical dynamics to recover learned semantic attractors. As in the previous section, sparsity is increased to isolate patterns corresponding to individual concepts (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_b} \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{c}\DIFaddend ). These patterns are then projected to MTL-semantic through initially random connections. As a result, receptive fields in MTL-semantic come to represent individual concepts identified by CTX (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_b}d). Consequently, while MTL-sensory exhibits a mixed selectivity to different concepts, MTL-semantic neurons show an absolute selectivity to these concepts (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_b}d, e), with mutual information becoming maximal rapidly after semantic replay is initiated (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_b}f). \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{This reflects not only that the cortical semantic dictionary is maximally informative about episodic latent factors, but that the cortical code is effectively transferred into MTL-semantic.
}\DIFaddend \newline\newline
Once MTL-semantic has acquired concept-selective receptive fields, replay can engage both MTL-sensory and MTL-semantic populations through recurrent MTL connectivity (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_b_supp}). As a result, subsequent replay events now contain both sensory and semantic information, inherited from CTX. While semantic representations are identical in content in CTX and MTL, they serve different purposes. In CTX, recurrent connectivity between semantic assemblies stores long-term predictiveness across concepts. However, in MTL-semantic, recurrent connectivity stores the  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{semantic associations of the }\DIFaddend episodes stored during  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the }\DIFaddend day. Extending replay in MTL to joint sensory-semantic episodes expands the previous CTX$\leftarrow$MTL-sensory connections to form cortical receptive fields that jointly respond to MTL-sensory and MTL-semantic patterns (Fig. \ref{fig:circuit}d).
 \newline\newline
Overall, these results show that semantic replay implements semantic-to-episodic consolidation in the circuit model, as proposed in the model. This provides a mechanistic account of how learned semantic representations can be transferred from neocortex to MTL, giving rise to concept-like cells  \DIFaddbegin \shortcite{quiroga_2005, quiroga_2012}\DIFaddend , and providing CTX and MTL with a shared vocabulary of semantic representations.

\subsection*{Variable sparsity during replay controls the complexity of the vocabulary learned}
So far, episodic replay has been constrained to high sparsity, a regime that decorrelates episodic subpatterns and promotes the extraction of \textit{simple concepts} (e.g. tomato or cheese). However, if replay is allowed to recruit a larger number of active neurons (decreasing sparsity), the network can in principle recover patterns corresponding to higher-order combinations of concepts. For example, replay may recover conjunctions of concepts such as \textit{crust}, \textit{cheese}, and \textit{tomato} by allowing a less sparse pattern to emerge (Fig. \ref{fig:higher-order}a).
\newline\newline
To test this idea, we  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{relax the previous assumption of the model that replay is necessarily sparse. Instead, we }\DIFaddend define the \textit{semantic load} \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{of a replay event }\DIFaddend as the ratio between the number of active neurons  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{in a replay event and the minimum number permitted in that region, with previous sections corresponding to a semantic load of 1. Given that a semantic load of 1 tunes cortical neurons to simple concepts such as $A_1$, we call this minimum number $K^\textrm{region}_\textrm{concept}$ (see Methods). This allows re-interpreting the number of active neurons in a region $K^\textrm{region}(t) = S_L(t)\cdot K^\textrm{region}_\textrm{concept}$ as the co-activation of $S_L(t)$ neuronal assemblies, each representing a well-defined concept. Thus, the intuition of the semantic load is to control }\DIFaddend how many concepts are simultaneously expressed in a replayed pattern. In  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{our model}\DIFaddend , replay events have a semantic load of either 1 or 2 (Fig. \ref{fig:higher-order}b). Consequently, MTL attractor dynamics recover a pattern associated with either a single concept (e.g. $A_1$) or a conjunction of two concepts (such as $A_1B_2$), depending on how many neurons are allowed to remain active. The resulting MTL activity is then projected to CTX, where a separate cortical subregion learns these higher-order  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{associations}\DIFaddend .
\newline\newline
Under this regime, replay with a semantic load \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{of }\DIFaddend 2 induces a competition between neurons coding for specific combinations $(A_i,B_j)$, that is, complete episodic conjunctions rather than isolated concepts. Over time, CTX develops selective receptive fields for these combinations (Figs. \ref{fig:higher-order} \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{b--d}\DIFaddend , \textit{complex concepts}, and Fig. \ref{fig:circuit}d). As a result, cortical activity during wake contains neurons tuned to each $A_i$, to each $B_j$, and to specific conjunctions $(A_i,B_j)$ (Fig. \ref{fig:higher-order}c). In this way, cortical dictionary learning extends from simple cortical words (\textit{atoms} represent simple concepts) to higher-order compositional words (\textit{atoms} represent complex concepts).
\newline\newline
When examining CTX neurons selective to complex concepts, we find that learning reorganizes activity into a conjunctive code, at each timestep representing an abstracted individual episode $A_iB_j$ (Fig. \ref{fig:higher-order}d). Consistently, the mutual information between these neurons and  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{conjunctions of simple concepts, of }\DIFaddend the form $A_iB_j$\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, }\DIFaddend increases sharply once variable sparsity is introduced (Fig. \ref{fig:higher-order}e). By contrast, the normalized mutual information  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{between }\DIFaddend CTX simple-concept neurons  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{or }\DIFaddend MTL-sensory activity  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{and }\DIFaddend these complex latent  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{values }\DIFaddend remains notably below maximal (Fig. \ref{fig:higher-order}e). Similarly to the case of simple concept vocabulary learning, the formation of new receptive fields is favoured by  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{a higher probability }\DIFaddend of the corresponding episode being sampled (Fig. \ref{fig:higher-order}f).
\newline\newline
This transformation is also reflected in corticocortical connectivity. As higher-order cortical words form, CTX recurrent synapses encode the relational structure between simple concepts and their conjunctions (Fig. \ref{fig:circuit}e). It should be noted that the asymmetry of this structure is inherited by the connectivity matrix: a conjunction such as $A_1B_1$ strongly predicts its constituent $A_1$, whereas $A_1$ only weakly predicts the specific conjunction $A_1B_1$ (Fig. \ref{fig:circuit}e). Consequently, recurrent connectivity can encode directional predictive relationships, overcoming a limitation of many Hebbian models that are restricted to symmetric relationships such as correlation.
\newline\newline
Together, these results show that replay sparsity controls the level of abstraction learned by the cortical dictionary. Highly sparse replay favours isolated concepts, whereas less sparse replay allows CTX to consolidate higher-order cortical words corresponding to concept conjunctions. In the pizza example, replay may first extract individual ingredients such as \textit{cheese}, \textit{tomato}, or \textit{crust}, and later consolidate pizza as their conjunction, capturing increasingly episodic-like semantic structure.

\subsection*{The model reproduces key neural and synaptic experimental signatures}
 We next examine whether the circuit emerging from the proposed learning algorithm reproduces key experimental observations. To this end, we compare the representations, connectivity, and learning dynamics of the model against a range of experimental findings focusing on the neocortical-hippocampal dialogue.
\subsubsection*{Formation of concept cells in MTL}
We first test whether semantic structure in the model reshapes medial temporal coding in a manner consistent with the defining properties of concept cells, namely concept-level representations that generalize across contexts (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}a). A central commitment of the model is that semantic abstraction is represented within the episodic circuit itself, rather than emerging only at a downstream cortical readout. This predicts that MTL populations should support concept-level generalization across contexts. Consistent with this, concept information is more readily decoded across held-out contexts from MTL-semantic than from MTL-sensory (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}b1). At the single-neuron level, MTL-semantic units also show stronger selectivity to simple concepts (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}b2) and carry more information about their preferred concept (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}b3).
\subsubsection*{ \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Consolidation increases }\DIFaddend representational overlap  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{among }\DIFaddend memories with shared concepts , \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{both }\DIFaddend in CTX  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{and }\DIFaddend MTL}
We next ask whether consolidation reshapes representational geometry in a manner consistent with the model's overlap-based replay mechanism. Because replay is structured by shared  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{representations across episodes}\DIFaddend , the model predicts that memories sharing latent structure should become more similar over consolidation, both in hippocampus and in cortex.  A related effect was reported by \citeA{tompary_2017} in an object-scene paradigm, in which multiple object memories shared a smaller number of scene associations. The authors measured representational similarity during object-cued recall before and after sleep for objects associated with overlapping or non-overlapping scenes. Importantly, this task maps naturally onto our episode generation protocol, with $A_i$ corresponding to objects and $B_j$ to scenes (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}c). Consistent with the experimental findings, consolidation increases the overlap index in cortex, such that objects sharing a scene become represented more similarly after sleep than before sleep (Fig.  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\ref{fig:validation_circuit}d1}\DIFaddend ). Importantly, a corresponding increase is also observed in the model's MTL (Fig.  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\ref{fig:validation_circuit}d2}\DIFaddend ).
\subsubsection*{Engram-specific potentiation of CTX recurrent synapses, and cortex to MTL synapses}
Finally, we ask whether the particular neural and synaptic changes generated by the model could be related to experimentally described circuit motifs. Recent work \shortcite{lee_2023} characterized synaptic and representational changes during consolidation within a contextual fear-conditioning task, offering a particularly useful benchmark for our model because it further constrains the underlying circuit mechanisms. These experiments focused on interactions between the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC)\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, which we map onto the neocortex (CTX) in the model, }\DIFaddend and basolateral amygdala (BLA), which we map onto the  medial temporal lobe (MTL)  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{in the }\DIFaddend model. To capture the experimental paradigm within our framework, we interpret the latent pair $(A_i,B_j)$ in a contextual fear-conditioning framework: different $A_i$ represent sensory descriptions of context, with  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$A_1$ }\DIFaddend serving as the conditioned stimulus (CS\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{; $A_1$}\DIFaddend ), whereas $B_j$  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{represent }\DIFaddend internal valence states, including fear as the unconditioned stimulus (US; $B_1$) (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}f1). Conditioning is then modeled by presenting the CS  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{together with random activity within neurons coding for $B$}\DIFaddend , followed within the same day by its pairing with the fear state \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(consistent presentation of $A_1B_1$)}\DIFaddend .
\newline\newline
\citeA{lee_2023} examined how remote contextual fear memories are represented by distinguishing neurons active during conditioning from those active during later recall. In their terminology, \emph{engram neurons} are neurons recruited during the original fear-conditioning episode, whereas \emph{recall neurons} are neurons recruited during remote memory retrieval. These populations only partially overlap: some recall neurons are reactivated engram neurons, whereas others are newly recruited at recall. The model reproduces this reorganization of cortical recruitment \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{following consolidation }\DIFaddend (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}f2).
\newline\newline
When examining the connectivity motifs, the model develops stronger recurrent synapses among cortical engram neurons than between engram and non-engram neurons, reproducing the selective strengthening of engram-defined cortical connectivity after consolidation (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}g1). The model likewise reproduces stronger projections from cortical engram neurons to medial temporal engram neurons than  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{to }\DIFaddend non-engram  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{medial temporal }\DIFaddend neurons (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}g2), as well as stronger inputs from recall-recruited cortical neurons onto cortical engram cells than from non-recall neurons (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}g3). In the model, the strengthening of CTX$\rightarrow$MTL projections arises from semantic replay, which repeatedly reactivates cortical semantic assemblies and transfers their structure to MTL. Thus, the experimentally observed increase from the newly formed cortical engram to BLA is consistent with the prediction that semantic knowledge is progressively incorporated into episodic representations through cortex-to-MTL teaching.
\newline\newline
Lee  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{and colleagues }\DIFaddend next asked about the impact of extinction on the synaptic engram  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}h). }\DIFaddend In particular, they compared engram-to-engram and engram-to-non-engram cortical synapses after fear conditioning followed by extinction, and found that the strong synaptic contrast observed after consolidation was no longer clearly maintained  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}i, original experiment). However, while the model reproduces behavioural }\DIFaddend extinction (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit} \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{j), and consistently experiences a decrease in the E-E connectivity (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit_supp}a), the cortical representations }\DIFaddend formed during consolidation \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{are not fully erased (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}i)}\DIFaddend .  As a result, extinction suppresses recall expression without fully erasing the underlying representational scaffold, providing  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{an }\DIFaddend explanation for the enhanced re-learning (savings) observed when conditioning is reintroduced after an initial conditioning-extinction sequence (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}k). \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{It should also be noted that the difference with experimental reported results can be explained when the number of synapses is reduced to match original measurements (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit_supp}b).
}\DIFaddend \subsubsection*{ \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Link between specific model }\DIFaddend mechanisms and reproduced results}
Together, these results link the core mechanisms of the model to experimentally observed neural and synaptic signatures. The cortical dictionary learning is consistent with consolidation-related increases in overlap of conceptually related memories \shortcite{tompary_2017} and the reorganization of cortical recruitment observed between encoding and remote recall \shortcite{lee_2023}. Similarly, while standard consolidation theories predict catastrophic forgetting following extinction, our model explains savings in  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{re-acquisition }\DIFaddend as an update of the semantic structure across previously existing cortical entries (embedded in recurrent cortical connections). In turn, the transfer of semantic representations into the episodic circuit is supported by the formation of concept cells \shortcite{quiroga_2005, quiroga_2012} and the fact that consolidation promotes overlap across conceptually similar memories not only in cortex, but also in hippocampus \shortcite{tompary_2017}. Finally, the strengthening of corticofugal (MTL$\leftarrow$CTX) connections from cortical recurrent assemblies recruited during consolidation \shortcite{lee_2023} is consistent with the idea that recurrent-driven spontaneous cortical activity can shape representations in downstream subcortical areas (\textit{semantic replay}).

\subsection*{Normative benefits of semantic representations in episodic memory}
The emergence of semantic representations in the model’s medial temporal lobe allows us to ask what normative role semantic structure plays in episodic memory . This question revisits a longstanding tension: optimal attractor dynamics favor orthogonal representations, yet episodic representations in the medial temporal lobe are richly structured and semantically organized. Furthermore, rather than being detrimental, semantic information can facilitate recall and is usually more easily remembered than sensory detail. Our model provides a framework to examine why such structure might be beneficial, and what computational trade-offs it introduces for recall, replay, and consolidation.
\newline\newline
To isolate the contribution of semantic representations, we compare three network modes (Fig. \ref{fig:normative}a). In the \textit{semantics present} condition (Fig. \ref{fig:normative}a, green), the model operates normally and MTL-semantic contains concept-level representations learned from CTX. In the \textit{semantics random} condition (Fig. \ref{fig:normative}a, gray), MTL-semantic remains active but its activity is replaced by random patterns at each timestep, controlling for the presence of additional neural activity without semantic content. Finally, in the \textit{semantics absent} condition (Fig. \ref{fig:normative}a, orange), MTL-semantic is excluded from MTL attractor dynamics altogether, corresponding to a purely sensory episodic system. Together, these conditions can isolate the effects of semantic information from the effects of increasing the size of the episodic memory network.
\subsubsection*{Semantic representations in episodic memory increase memory capacity when episodes are semantically in-distribution}
We often recall events that we can semantically comprehend much better than those we cannot  \DIFaddbegin \shortcite{chase_1973, murray_1976, lin_2021}\DIFaddend . This typically leads to an effective amnesia for what we cannot semantically process (as introduced in  \DIFaddbegin \shortciteA{tse_2007}\DIFaddend ). To understand what mechanism might lead to such an effect, we begin by establishing a baseline in which no semantic structure is present. Specifically, we compare recall of purely random sparse patterns in our medial temporal lobe network against a classical sparse Hopfield network with matched size and sparsity (Fig. \ref{fig:normative}b). Under these conditions, our network shows a minor advantage at low memory loads, followed by a memory cliff as load increases. Thus, the model exhibits the standard overload regime expected for associative storage of random, unstructured patterns.
\newline\newline
We then examine recall as the number of stored patterns increases when episodes are sampled from our standard structured distribution (Fig. \ref{fig:normative}c), across different levels of input Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR). One striking difference  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{from }\DIFaddend unstructured pattern storage, common across all networks, is the presence of a U-shaped  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{curve with the number of stored patterns}\DIFaddend . Performance initially decreases as more episodes are stored, but later recovers as repeated exposure to the same underlying structure allows recurrent weights to average out sensory noise.
\newline\newline
Notwithstanding that, this recovery is weaker when semantic representations are absent or randomly assigned (compare recall for high number of stored patterns across networks, Fig. \ref{fig:normative}c). Without semantic representations, the dip is more pronounced, whereas networks with aligned semantic representations retain higher recall. The advantage of semantic representations (measured as the difference across networks) also increases with sensory noise (Fig. \ref{fig:normative}c). This suggests MTL-semantic provides an additional, structured population that compresses the episode into concept-level variables. This increases the number of neurons available to represent the episodic semantic content, providing an error-correcting scaffold for MTL-sensory activity. As a result, semantic representations  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{can improve recall }\DIFaddend of the sensory component.
\newline\newline
Finally, within the \textit{semantics present} network, recall from MTL-semantic is substantially higher than recall from MTL-sensory (Fig. \ref{fig:normative}c, compare  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{solid }\DIFaddend (MTL-sensory recall) and  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{dashed }\DIFaddend (MTL-semantic recall) lines). This is expected because MTL-semantic represents the lower-dimensional conceptual structure of the episode using its own dedicated neural population, whereas MTL-sensory must preserve more variable perceptual detail. This provides a circuit-level account of why the semantic content of memories is often more robustly recalled than their sensory details, which are more prone to being distorted or forgotten.
\newline\newline
Together, these results suggest that semantic structure optimizes the recall capacity of episodic memory by finding a compressed code that effectively amplifies the signal of the episodic conceptual structure. When stored patterns are random, the model behaves like a standard associative memory and eventually fails through overload. When episodes are semantically in-distribution, however, overlap is no longer purely interfering, and instead reflects reusable structure that MTL-semantic can encode to stabilize recall. Thus, semantic representations do not generically increase memory capacity; rather, they increase effective capacity when new episodes can be interpreted through the learned semantic vocabulary.
\subsubsection*{Semantic representations in episodic memory induce a \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{compositional }\DIFaddend generalization bias in replayed and consolidated representations}
Following recent theoretical studies proposing that replay optimizes generalization in Complementary Learning Systems (GO-CLS, \citeA{sun_2023}), we hypothesize that semantic representations can play a role in the generalization of representations consolidated via replay. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Thus, the benefit of semantic representations in episodic memory might not be limited to episodic recall, but even affect the semantization of future episodes that are compositions of previously learned abstractions.
}\DIFaddend \newline\newline
First, we investigate whether the presence of semantic content during episodic encoding affects the generalization of concepts that are compositions of previously learned concepts (compositional generalization). We compare the test-time accuracy of the three network modes introduced above in classifying \textit{complex concepts} from cortical activity, after increasing the semantic load to 2 and training for  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{400 }\DIFaddend additional days. As the signal-to-noise ratio of the sensory input (SNR input) decreases, the gap in compositional generalization between networks with present and absent (or random) semantic representations increases (Fig. \ref{fig:normative}d). This gap persists at intermediate noise levels, until the  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{accuracies of both converge }\DIFaddend to chance.
\newline\newline
To understand the origin of this cortical generalization gap, we next examine the replayed representations themselves. The first measure we obtain is the signal-to-noise ratio of the replayed pattern (SNR replayed). This is done by inferring which input-generation SNR would produce a pattern like the replayed one, considering only MTL-sensory. Semantic representations consistently increase the SNR associated with the replayed patterns, coinciding with the region in which there is a generalization gap (Figs. \ref{fig:normative}d and \ref{fig:normative}e). However, when explicitly testing the generalization of replayed MTL-sensory against awake patterns (Methods), the effect only partially explains the gap (Figs. \ref{fig:normative} \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{f and \ref{fig:normative_supp}a}\DIFaddend ). Although the initially formed receptive field is slightly better at identifying future semantically equivalent episodes, this does not fully correlate with the generalization gap during testing. We  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{additionally }\DIFaddend measure replayed/replayed generalization: how  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{good }\DIFaddend are replayed patterns \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, when used as receptive fields, at classifying future replayed patterns? }\DIFaddend (Methods, Fig. \ref{fig:normative} \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{g) }\DIFaddend This measure is more strongly correlated with compositional generalization (Fig.  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\ref{fig:normative_supp}b}\DIFaddend ).
\newline\newline
These results can be interpreted in light of two slightly different mechanisms potentially driving better generalization after learning: when an episodic pattern is replayed, MTL is projected to CTX, and the activated cortical neurons update their MTL receptive fields in the direction of the replayed medial temporal pattern. Therefore, the formation of cortical representations during replay depends on two factors: (i) the similarity of the replayed MTL-sensory pattern to semantically equivalent future \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{sensory }\DIFaddend inputs and (ii) whether the \textit{right} cortical neurons are activated, which depends on the generalization across replayed patterns. That the second factor is more important for post-training generalization is consistent with the idea that cortical representations are not determined by any single replay event, but instead emerge gradually through the accumulation of many replay-driven updates under a small learning rate. As a consequence, even if semantic structure also makes individual replayed MTL-sensory patterns less noisy, the dominant factor for cortical generalization in the model is whether the \textit{appropriate} cortical neurons are consistently recruited across replay events.
\newline\newline
These results suggest that semantic representations promote cortical generalization not only by denoising replayed medial temporal patterns, but by providing a low-dimensional scaffold that remains tightly coordinated with cortex and helps constrain which cortical neurons are activated during replay. Together, our findings support the view that a shared semantic vocabulary across medial temporal lobe and neocortex can shape episodic replay into a teaching signal that is inherently more suitable for forming abstract, robust, and generalizable cortical representations.

\subsection*{Semantic representations in the episodic memory circuit explain a range of behavioral phenomena}
We finally ask whether the properties of episodic attractor dynamics characterized above  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{can }\DIFaddend explain a broader range of behavioral phenomena (Fig. \ref{fig:behavioural}). 
\newline\newline
In  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the previous section}\DIFaddend , we show how semantic MTL representations structure episodic memory to benefit recall and consolidation dynamics 
 \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(Fig. \ref{fig:normative}). }\DIFaddend This should be consistent with behavioural studies showing that semantically interpretable episodes are more faithfully recalled, for example when comparing \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{real vs randomized chess positions }\shortcite{chase_1973}\DIFadd{, }\DIFaddend actual words with scrambled letters \shortcite{murray_1976}, or intact images with phase-scrambled ones \shortcite{lin_2021} (Fig. \ref{fig:behavioural}a). This result lies at the core of the aforementioned tension between theory (patterns should be separated  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{into a content-agnostic code}\DIFaddend ) and experiments (structured episodic overlap is beneficial for recall). To reproduce this effect, we compare the frozen network's recall of standard patterns (\textit{semantics intact}) to its recall of fixed permutations of the same patterns (\textit{semantics scrambled}) (see Methods). The recall performance of patterns that can be semantically interpreted is significantly higher than that of semantically scrambled versions (Fig. \ref{fig:behavioural} \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{b}\DIFaddend ), even though the generated episodes are equivalent except for sensory neuronal identity.
\newline\newline
Another key difference between the model and previous accounts of semantic consolidation is that the episodic memory system performs an initial generalization step, finding structure across the different stored episodes in the medial temporal lobe.  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{In our model, however, }\DIFaddend replayed activity is consistently de-noised with respect to sensory input  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(Fig. \ref{fig:normative}e). }\DIFaddend This predicts that enforcing a consistent overlapping component  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{episodes within a block }\DIFaddend benefits the formation of that particular semantic representation, which has previously been shown in humans in the literature on \textit{blocked} versus \textit{interleaved} learning \shortcite{flesch_2018}. Importantly, this result goes against standard neural network accounts of learning, and a circuit account using only biologically plausible synaptic plasticity has yet to be proposed. We therefore test a similar protocol, training  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{two networks from scratch}\DIFaddend : one with \textit{blocked training} (all episodes in a day contain a fixed $A_i$ or $B_j$), and one with \textit{interleaved training}, where every episode is equally likely (Fig. \ref{fig:behavioural}c, see Methods). Across noise levels, blocked learning shows a significant advantage over interleaved learning (Fig. \ref{fig:behavioural}d, right), replicating previous results (Fig. \ref{fig:behavioural}d, left). The model suggests that  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{blockwise learning }\DIFaddend promotes the consolidation of cortical representations for the  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{elements within a block. This mechanism is difficult to explain with models that simply rearrange }\DIFaddend cortical recurrent connectivity  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{toward }\DIFaddend replayed targets, a process  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{prone to }\DIFaddend catastrophic forgetting.
\newline\newline
Finally, we  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{use the model }\DIFaddend to reproduce semantic memory deficits linked to lesioning of the episodic memory system. In particular, we aim to confirm the standard anterograde semantic amnesia (lesioning MTL before consolidation impairs semantic recall) and, more importantly, retrograde semantic amnesia \shortcite{duff_2020}. Retrograde semantic amnesia is harder to explain with classic models of semantic consolidation, because the role of MTL is only to hash cortical representations.
\newline\newline
We first perform a standard anterograde lesion test: a new association is encoded, after which MTL recurrent and replay dynamics are disrupted before consolidation. Under this manipulation, later cortical completion of the associated  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{concepts }\DIFaddend is markedly reduced relative to sham networks (Fig. \ref{fig:behavioural} \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{e}\DIFaddend ). Then, we test whether MTL-semantic also contributes to the retrieval of already consolidated semantic structure. To do so, we allow a new association to be encoded and consolidated, and only then lesion the semantic MTL pathway. We then probe cortical recall from partial cues while progressively increasing cortical noise. Sham networks can still recover the semantically related representation, whereas lesioned networks show a much steeper loss of semantically related cortical activity (Fig. \ref{fig:behavioural}f). Thus, while the model matches the classical role of MTL in new learning (Fig. \ref{fig:behavioural}e), it also captures an effect that standard one-way consolidation models fail to explain: semantic representations in MTL can continue to support semantic recall even after cortical learning has occurred (Fig. \ref{fig:behavioural}f).
\newline\newline
Together, these simulations show that the same structured overlap that improves episodic storage also reproduces behavioural effects that are difficult to explain with standard consolidation models. First, the incorporation of semantic representations into episodic memory provides a natural explanation for the well-established advantage of semantically interpretable stimuli during recall, despite the increased overlap such representations introduce between memories  \DIFaddbegin \shortcite{chase_1973, murray_1976, lin_2021}\DIFaddend . Second, the replay-based dictionary learning as a mechanism for consolidation predicts that learning experiences sharing a common  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{factor value }\DIFaddend should accelerate concept formation, reproducing the behavioural benefits of blocked over interleaved learning \shortcite{flesch_2018}. Finally, the presence of semantic representations within the episodic memory system explains why medial temporal lobe damage can impair not only the acquisition of new semantic knowledge but also the retrieval of previously consolidated semantic information \shortcite{duff_2020}.

\section*{Discussion}
We have developed a model of episodic-semantic memory interactions based on two complementary principles. First, that the algorithm underlying semantic consolidation can be viewed as a form of dictionary learning over episodic experience. Second, that this learned vocabulary can be transferred back into episodic memory, providing both memory systems with a shared semantic vocabulary. We have proposed a computational circuit of the model, where the interactions of episodic and semantic memory are implemented via interactions of a Medial Temporal Lobe (MTL) and a neocortex (CTX) network. The circuit, based on Hebbian local plasticity, uses episodic replay as the main mechanism to perform dictionary learning over compositional sensory input from MTL to CTX. Similarly, spontaneous  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{cortical }\DIFaddend activity enables a transfer of CTX representations into MTL, shaping the episodic memory code as proposed by our model. The same circuit model provides mechanistic insights into the role of semantic representations in episodic memory, suggesting that they act as an error-correcting scaffold that improves recall and stabilizes further consolidation dynamics as long as new episodes remain semantically  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{in-distribution}\DIFaddend .
\newline\newline
A first contribution of this work is to recast semantic consolidation as a form of dictionary learning. Classical consolidation theories propose that replay enables the gradual emergence of semantic knowledge from episodic experience \shortcite{mcclelland_1995, oreilly_2014}, with many computational implementations relying on supervised or error-driven learning mechanisms to achieve this transformation \shortcite{saxe_2019, singh_2022, sun_2023, spens_2024}. In contrast, our framework suggests that replay may provide candidate structure from which neocortex discovers a set of reusable representations capable of explaining recurring patterns across experiences. This is consistent with growing evidence that replay is selective, reconstructive, and generative rather than a faithful reinstantiation of prior activity \shortcite{swanson_2020, diekmann_2023}. More broadly, this perspective provides a unified account of two complementary functions often attributed to systems consolidation: the long-term stabilization of individual episodic memories and the extraction of abstract knowledge across experiences. Existing computational work has largely treated these objectives separately, with high-level models focusing on semantic abstraction and generalization, and biologically grounded circuit models primarily addressing the stabilization of individual memories, often inspired by contextual fear conditioning paradigms. Our results suggest that these could be different outcomes of the same replay-driven learning process, depending on the episodic complexity of the word (or \textit{atom}) formed. This could account for personal semantics \shortcite{renoult_2012}, when the concept formed corresponds to an intermediate level of complexity (e.g. the concept of someone's workspace), supporting a continuum of consolidation, from purely episodic to highly abstract.
\newline\newline
A second central finding of this work is a mechanism for transferring abstract representations from one sub-network to another. In the model, spontaneous activity in neocortex shapes medial temporal representations, increasing their level of abstraction during episodic encoding. The idea that neocortex can transfer semantic representations to subcortical areas has inspired several computational models in recent years. For example, cortex-initiated generative replay has been proposed to align cortical and hippocampal representations \shortcite{van_2020}\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, offering an alternative explanation for how MTL representations keep track of cortical representational shift }\shortcite{kali_2004}\DIFaddend . Similarly, this assumption underlies models in which cortical-extracted regularities can be used for episodic compression \shortcite{spens_2024_b, nagy_2025} and by the episodic pattern completion machinery \shortcite{whittington_2020, kang_2023, spens_2024, kang_2024}. All of these proposals have in turn been inspired by experimental findings supporting the presence of semantic representations in the episodic memory circuit \shortcite{quiroga_2005, quiroga_2012, aronov_2017, sun_2020}.  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{While previous work has proposed that semantic representations are first extracted by the hippocampal circuit, and then used by neocortex }\shortcite{gluck_1993}\DIFadd{, }\DIFaddend converging lines of evidence  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{suggest neocortex might actively influence }\DIFaddend hippocampal replay and subsequent consolidation \shortcite{remondes_2004, rothschild_2017, de_sousa_2026}. Recent work also points in this direction \shortcite{wang_2026}, showing that shortly after learning the temporal  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{structure of ripples changes }\DIFaddend from hippocampus leading (here called episodic replay) to neocortex leading (here called semantic replay), in agreement with our model's prediction. However, a biologically plausible circuit model of this process has yet to be proposed. While still hypothetical, our results provide a mechanistic account of the formation of semantic representations in episodic memory relying only on local Hebbian plasticity. Furthermore, the model's mechanism is consistent with experimental findings in which neocortical recurrent engrams serve as a substrate for further synaptic potentiation of downstream corticofugal connections \shortcite{lee_2023}, in particular from prefrontal cortex (PFC) to basolateral amygdala (BLA), which we reproduce here.
\newline\newline
Beyond providing a mechanistic account of how semantic representations emerge and become incorporated into episodic memory, our results suggest a normative role for semantic structure within episodic codes. Classical theories of associative memory have long emphasized the importance of sparse and decorrelated representations for maximizing storage capacity \shortcite{hopfield_1982, tsodyks_1988, treves_1991}. From this perspective, semantic overlap between memories would be expected to introduce interference and impair retrieval. Far from being  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{detrimental}\DIFaddend , semantic representations can in fact benefit episodic memory \shortcite{murray_1976, tse_2007, lin_2021}. Our results offer one possible explanation for this apparent tension. By providing abstract representations that capture regularities across experiences, semantic concepts act as an error-correcting scaffold during memory retrieval, allowing partially degraded episodic patterns to be reconstructed more accurately. Importantly, this benefit extends beyond recall itself. Because replay relies on the reactivation of stored episodic representations, improved attractor dynamics also lead to more reliable replay events and an increased signal-to-noise ratio during consolidation. In this view, semantic representations do not merely coexist with episodic memories but actively improve their stability, retrieval, and communication with neocortex. More broadly, these findings suggest that semantization may represent a computational trade-off in which a modest reduction in memory specificity is compensated by substantial gains in robustness, generalization, and consolidation efficiency, provided that new experiences remain semantically  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{in-distribution}\DIFaddend .
\newline\newline
Taken together, our results aim to reconcile the generally assumed one-way consolidation of circuit models of systems consolidation \shortcite{oreilly_2014,kumaran_2016} with a rich body of experiments demonstrating that semantic knowledge shapes episodic memory. These include reconstructive and schema-dependent recall \shortcite{bartlett_1932}, false memories for semantically related items in the DRM paradigm \shortcite{deese_1959,roediger_1995}, expertise-dependent memory \shortcite{chase_1973}, and improved recall for semantically congruent episodes \shortcite{zoellner_2023}. Recent computational models have successfully captured several of these phenomena through semantic compression \shortcite{nagy_2020} or semantic completion of partial episodic traces \shortcite{fayyaz_2022}. In contrast, our work proposes an explicit  circuit mechanism based on replay, local plasticity, and bidirectional consolidation, providing a biologically grounded framework in which these semantic effects can emerge. While the specific mechanisms remain to be experimentally validated, we hope this model provides a useful circuit-level scaffold for generating mechanistic hypotheses that bridge psychological phenomena and systems-level learning theories.
\newline\newline
Our framework generates several experimentally testable predictions. In an effort to map our episode generation protocol to experiments in rodents, we propose a paradigm for compositional cue ($A_i$ visual, $B_j$ odour)-reward associations (Figure \ref{fig:predictions_supp}). We propose one prediction for each of the three key mechanisms studied here. First, if dictionary learning is the substrate for semantic learning, we anticipate that the number of cells tuned to the different components $A_i$ and $B_j$ will increase monotonically with the marginal probability of that  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{concept being present }\DIFaddend in any episode (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_a}g). A parallel prediction is that holographic manipulation of cortical abstract cells could reflect semantic structure (predictiveness) across concepts, thereby encoding the \textit{world model} in recurrent connections (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_a}h). This would support our mechanism for the slow extraction of semantics in CTX. Second, if semantic representations are transferred back into episodic memory through semantic replay, disrupting cortical ( \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{with prefrontal cortex being as a plausible candidate}\DIFaddend ) activity during consolidation should reduce the abstractness \DIFaddbegin \shortcite{bernardi_2020} \DIFaddend of medial temporal lobe representations  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{regarding the different factor values (concepts) }\DIFaddend presented to the animal. Finally, the model predicts that memories composed of previously learned concepts should consolidate more rapidly than entirely novel experiences. Behaviourally, this would imply that learning the reward association to a novel visual-odour $(A_i, B_j)$ cue would be faster when each individual cue has previously been learned than when the cues are novel (Fig. \ref{fig:normative}d).
\newline\newline
Several limitations of the present work point to promising directions for future research. First, the model intentionally abstracts away much of the anatomical and physiological complexity of hippocampal-neocortical interactions. While this simplification allows us to isolate the main underlying computational principles in a tractable manner, future work could investigate how these mechanisms interact, for example, with the rich circuitry of the medial temporal lobe, including the distinct contributions of entorhinal cortex, hippocampal subregions, or amygdala. Second, our implementation focuses on static representations and does not explicitly address the temporal structure of episodic memories. Extending the framework to sequences and temporally extended experiences is crucial to understand how the abstraction of dynamically changing  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{environments }\DIFaddend is implemented by the brain. Third, while we study a regime without imposed pattern separation in the hippocampal code  \shortcite{sun_2023, chandra_2025} , it is likely that memories involve both structured overlapping representations and pattern-separated hashing codes \shortcite{chettih_2024}. The computational mechanisms supporting the flexible use of both during encoding and retrieval have yet to be established. Finally, from a theory of learning perspective, the model could serve as an inspiration for brain-inspired machine learning algorithms, in which semantic and episodic memory conceptualizations are becoming increasingly relevant \shortcite{fountas_2025, dong_2025, pink_2025}.  
\newline\newline
We hope that the present framework provides a foundation for investigating cortical and subcortical circuits as cooperative components that play a crucial role in the brain's  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{ability }\DIFaddend to simultaneously generalize and memorize. In this light, our work suggests \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{that }\DIFaddend episodic and semantic memory may  be best understood as two interacting representational systems that continuously learn models of one another.

\section*{Methods}\label{methods}
\subsection*{Episode Generation Protocol}
The Episode Generation Protocol (EGP) is a generative process that samples neuronal activity in the sensory layer. Our proposed EGP follows a similar approach to that described in \shortciteA{albesagonzalez_2025}, but is outlined here again for completeness:
\subsection*{Episodes,  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{latent factors, }\DIFaddend and  concepts}
All \textit{episodes} share a structure, such that every episode contains one \textit{episode concept}  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(value) per }\DIFaddend episode  \DIFaddbegin \textit{\DIFadd{latent factor}\DIFaddend }. This means that, given  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{latent factors }\DIFaddend $A$ and $B$, with each  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{latent factor }\DIFaddend representing a set of episode concepts  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$A_i\in A, B_j\in B$}\DIFaddend , then the set of all possible episodes is defined as:
 \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}
    \DIFadd{E = \left\{ (A_i, B_j) \mid A_i \in A, B_j \in B \right\}
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
In other words, an episode $e\in E$ is  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{an ordered set }\DIFaddend of episode concepts  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$A_i$, $B_j$ such that $A_i$ belongs to latent factor }\DIFaddend $A$ and element  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$B_j$ belongs to latent factor }\DIFaddend $B$. In this study we have fixed  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$A$ }\DIFaddend to have 5 possible values  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$A_i\in \{A_1, A_2, A_3, A_4, A_5\}$ }\DIFaddend and likewise for  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$B_j\in \{B_1, B_2, B_3, B_4, B_5\}$. However, the model is robust against variations in the number of concepts per latent factor (Fig. \ref{fig:parameter_sweep}d). 
}\DIFaddend \newline\newline
\subsection*{Episode Probability Distribution}
As a generative process, our EGP samples episodes as a preliminary step to sampling sensory input. We do this by fixing a probability distribution over episodes:
 \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}
    \DIFadd{1 = \sum_{A_i, B_j}P(e = (A_i, B_j)) 
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
such that  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$P(e = (A_i, B_j)) \equiv P(i,j)$ defines the probability }\DIFaddend of an episode with  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{factor values }\DIFaddend $(A_i, B_j)$ to be sampled when generating episodes.
\subsection*{Episode-Input Mapping}
Ultimately, the EGP samples sensory layer activity. For this reason, one also has to define how a sampled episode is mapped into sensory input $X^\textrm{SEN}$. Every concept $c$ in $A\cup B$ has a set of associated neurons $\textrm{SEN}_{c}$ in $X^\textrm{SEN}$ such that, given an episode $e$\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{,
}\DIFaddend \begin{equation}
     X^\textrm{SEN}_{i} = \begin{cases} 
      1 & \textrm{if} \;\; i \in \textrm{SEN}_{c} \;\;\textrm{and}\;\; c \in e\\ 
      0 & \textrm{else} \\
   \end{cases}
\end{equation}
In order to define $\textrm{SEN}_c$, we specify the number of  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{latent factors $N_\textrm{latent-factors}$}\DIFaddend , in this case 2 ($A$ and $B$). Then, we define the number of concepts  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(possible values) per latent factor }\DIFaddend ($|A|$ and $|B|$, here  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{both 5. Thus, }\DIFaddend $A_{i \in [1, 5]}$ and $B_{j \in [1, 5]}$). Then, we fix the number of neurons per concept $N_\textrm{concept}^A$ and $N_\textrm{concept}^B$, and define $\textrm{SEN}_{c}$ as:
 \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}
    \DIFadd{\textrm{SEN}_{c} = \{k : \textrm{start}(c) \leq k < \textrm{stop}(c)\}
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
with $\textrm{start}(c) = (i - 1) \cdot N_\textrm{concept}^A$ if $c = A_i \in A$ and $\textrm{start}(c) =|A|\cdot N_\textrm{concept}^A + (j - 1) \cdot N_\textrm{concept}^B$ if $c = B_j \in B$, and similarly $\textrm{stop}(c) = i \cdot N_\textrm{concept}^A$ if $c = A_i \in A$ and $\textrm{stop}(c) =|A|\cdot N_\textrm{concept}^A + j \cdot N_\textrm{concept}^B$ if $c = B_j \in B$ \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{. Intuitively, this can be thought of as a compositional extension of the standard orthogonal split of neuronal assemblies, with two neuronal pools of orthogonal representations being concatenated}\DIFaddend .
\newline\newline
To account for variability between different presentations of the same episode (not  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{every presentation of the same episode}\DIFaddend , even though  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{it contains }\DIFaddend the same concepts, will be exactly the same), we further add some stochasticity by randomly picking $N_\textrm{swap}$ inactive neurons and $N_\textrm{swap}$ active neurons and inverting their activity (a total of $2N_\textrm{swap}$ neurons randomly change their activity). This ensures that activity sparsity in the sensory layer is maintained (the number of swaps from 0 to 1 is the same as the number of swaps from 1 to 0). \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{An illustration of the episode-input mapping is included in the supplementary material (\ref{fig:input_generation}).
}\DIFaddend \subsubsection*{Input temporal structure}
Generated input $X_\textrm{input}$ has dimensions $(N_\textrm{days}, T_\textrm{day}, N_\textrm{SEN})$. Every day, a total of  $T_\textrm{day}/T_\textrm{episode}$ episodes (of $T_\textrm{episode}$ length) are sampled, and within each timestep an independent noise realization is sampled. 
\subsubsection*{Semantic Structure of an EGP}
Following \shortciteA{albesagonzalez_2025}, we refer to  \textit{semantic field theory} \shortcite{bussmann_2006} in order to define what is the \textit{semantic structure} of an EGP. According to this theory, the meaning of a word is not isolated but dependent on its relation to the rest of the words. While our task is not one of language, we can use this same paradigm to define the \textit{meaning} of episode concepts. In this sense, the meaning (semantics) of our episode concepts depends on how they are related to the rest (also note this is how semantics are established in a language dictionary, where each concept is defined via its relationship with the rest of \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the }\DIFaddend concepts). 
\newline\newline
Following this proposal, we use the conditional probabilities of being present in an episode between episode concepts as a proxy for the semantic structure of an EGP. In other words, extracting the semantics of an EGP is equivalent to \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(i) identifying the underlying concepts, and (ii) }\DIFaddend extracting how likely one  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{concept $c_i$ }\DIFaddend is to be present in an episode $e$ if  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{a concept $c_j$ }\DIFaddend is also present \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{in $e$}\DIFaddend . We define the Semantic Structure $\mathcal{SS}$ of an EGP with $N^\textrm{concepts}$ different concepts $c_i$, as a $N^\textrm{concepts}\times N^\textrm{concepts}$ matrix of the form:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{SS}_{ij} = P(c_i \in e|c_j \in e) 
    \label{eq:semantic_structure}
\end{equation}
For example, the intuition that pizza has a stronger semantic connection to cheese than cheese \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{does }\DIFaddend to pizza is reflected in their conditional probabilities (it is very common to have cheese in a pizza, but much less to have pizza in any dish containing cheese).  
\subsection*{Circuit Model}
\subsubsection*{Architecture and nomenclature}
\paragraph*{Regions and neural activity}
The circuit model contains 3 main regions: sensory cortex (SEN), neocortex (CTX), and medial temporal lobe (MTL). In turn, MTL is split into two sub-regions: MTL-sensory and MTL-semantic. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{CTX is likewise partitioned into two subregions CTX-simple and CTX-complex, which differ in number of neurons and neuronal excitability, and learn concepts at different levels of abstraction. }\DIFaddend We use $X$ to denote the neural activity, which is written as $X^\textrm{region}$ (for example $X^\textrm{CTX}$) when the region is to be made explicit. The same follows for pre-activation (synaptic) input, which is denoted by $\hat{X}$ (or $\hat{X}^\textrm{region})$. The activity of neuron $i$ in a region is $X^\textrm{region}_i$. The number of neurons in a region is denoted by $N_\textrm{region}$ (e.g. $N_\textrm{CTX}$).
\paragraph{Connectivity}
Connectivity matrices connecting a region \textrm{pre} to a region \textrm{post} take the form $W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}$, and a synapse connecting neuron $j$ in region pre with neuron $i$ in region post is $W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_{ij}$. For example, $W^{\textrm{CTX}\leftarrow\textrm{MTL}}_{13}$ represents the connection from neuron 3 in MTL to neuron 1 in CTX. Connectivity is classified as either feed-forward (connecting two different regions), which includes  $W^{\textrm{MTL-sensory}\leftarrow\textrm{SEN}}$, $W^{\textrm{CTX}\leftarrow\textrm{MTL}}$, and $W^{\textrm{MTL-semantic}\leftarrow\textrm{CTX}}$, or recurrent (connecting a region with itself), which includes $W^{\textrm{CTX}\leftarrow\textrm{CTX}}$, and $W^{\textrm{MTL}\leftarrow\textrm{MTL}}$. All connections are plastic except $W^{\textrm{MTL-sensory}\leftarrow\textrm{SEN}}$, which is fixed. Given a connectivity matrix $W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}$, we call \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the }\DIFaddend receptive field of a postsynaptic neuron $i$ $\textrm{RF}^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_i$ the vector representing the connections from region pre to postsynaptic neuron $i$
 \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}
    \DIFadd{\textrm{RF}^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_i =  W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_{i,:} = \{}{\DIFadd{W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}}}\DIFadd{_{ij}\}_j 
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
\paragraph{Synaptic Initialization}
\paragraph{Sensory cortex to MTL-sensory}
$W^{\textrm{MTL-sensory}\leftarrow\textrm{SEN}}$ is initialized as the identity, mapping to MTL-sensory an exact copy of $X^\textrm{SEN}$.
\paragraph{Feed-Forward connections}
 \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The rest of feed-forward }\DIFaddend connections are sampled from a Gaussian distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation $W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\sigma$ as a network parameter.
\begin{equation}
    W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\sigma)
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Recurrent connections}
Recurrent connections are initialized at 0. MTL connections are reset every \textit{day} (see Algorithm  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\ref{alg:wake}}\DIFaddend )
\paragraph{Mature and immature neurons}  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{CTX and MTL-semantic neurons (those with synapses that perform statistical learning) }\DIFaddend in the model can be either \textit{mature} or \textit{immature}. The intuition is: do these neurons contain meaningful representations via input received from another region (mature) or are mostly non-selective and random (immature)? More details on the dynamics and plasticity of mature and immature neurons can be seen below, but to summarize: (i) immature neurons have higher excitability during feed-forward input processing (not during pattern completion) and (ii) immature neurons have a higher learning rate  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{during sleep. Consequently, during replay, immature postsynaptic neurons tend to be recruited when the }\DIFaddend presynaptic pattern is  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{poorly matched by existing receptive fields}\DIFaddend . By doing this, if a presynaptic pattern is similar to previously formed representations, it slightly changes the already formed representations. If not, a set of immature neurons win the competition, develop receptive field due to \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{their }\DIFaddend higher plasticity, and then become mature. This allows one-shot formation of new postsynaptic representations of presynaptic input when an input pattern is too different from those presented to the network before, and then refining these representations with a slower learning rate.  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{CTX (outgoing) recurrent connections of immature neurons also benefit from higher plasticity rates during sleep}\DIFaddend . Maturity in a region is denoted by $\textrm{IM}^\textrm{region}$, where $\textrm{IM}^\textrm{region}_i = 1$ if neuron $i$ in the region is immature, and 0 otherwise.
\subsubsection*{Outgoing and incoming homeostasis across types of connections}
Below, we formalize two regimes of homeostatic plasticity: outgoing homeostasis and incoming homeostasis. Outgoing homeostasis guarantees that the sum of the outgoing connections from neuron $j$ in a connectivity matrix $W$ does not exceed a certain value $W_\textrm{max-out}$ ($\sum_iW_{ij} \leq W_\textrm{max-out} \forall j$). Similarly, incoming homeostasis guarantees a bound in incoming connections ($\sum_jW_{ij} \leq W_\textrm{max-in} \forall i$). Depending on the type of connectivity we choose:
\begin{itemize}
    \item outgoing homeostasis for CTX$\leftarrow$CTX: this makes the connectivity matrix reflect semantic structure as defined in \eqref{eq:semantic_structure}, as the connection from neuron coding from concept $j$ to neuron coding for concept $i$ becomes proportional to the conditional probability of neuron $i$ firing given neuron $j$ fires \shortcite{albesagonzalez_2025}
    \item incoming homeostasis  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{for }\DIFaddend feed-forward connectivity: in this homeostatic regime, connection from neuron $j$ to neuron $i$ becomes proportional to  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$p(j \; \textrm{active} | i\; \textrm{active})$ }\DIFaddend \shortcite{albesagonzalez_2025}. The \textit{trick} is that if an initial one-shot receptive field makes neuron $i$ selective to concept  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$c_i$}\DIFaddend , receptive fields approximate the average presynaptic activity pattern (prototype) given the concept  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$c_i$ }\DIFaddend is present (neuron $i$ is active), thus reinforcing higher selectivity in noisier regimes.
    \item Homeostatic reset in MTL$\leftarrow$MTL connections. In order to account for symmetric plasticity kernels in hippocampus \shortcite{mishra_2016, li_2023}, we  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{use standard }\DIFaddend Hebbian learning in MTL$\leftarrow$MTL. Homeostasis is obtained by resetting MTL recurrent connections to 0  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{at the start of }\textit{\DIFadd{wake}} \DIFadd{(Algorithm \ref{alg:wake})}\DIFaddend .
\end{itemize}
\subsection*{Network Operations}
\subsubsection*{Neuronal activation}
Synaptic input is mapped to neural activity via a \textit{K-winners-take-all} mechanism, in which the $K$ neurons with the highest pre-activation input $\hat{X}$ set their activity to 1, and the rest to 0. This can be written as
\begin{equation}
    X = \textrm{top-}K\big(\hat{X}\big)
\end{equation}
In  order to determine $K$ in a particular region \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(or subregion)}\DIFaddend , first we define
\begin{equation}
    K^\textrm{region}_\textrm{concept} \leq K^\textrm{region}_\textrm{episode}
\end{equation}
 such that each concept is represented by $K^\textrm{region}_\textrm{concept}$ neurons, and a typical episode during wakefulness contains $K^\textrm{region}_\textrm{episode}$ active neurons. In this context, given $K$ active neurons in a region, we define the semantic load $S_L$ to be the number of concepts represented by $K$ neurons, such that
 \begin{equation}
     K = S_L \cdot K^\textrm{region}_\textrm{concept}
     \label{eq:semantic_load}
 \end{equation}
In other words, by fixing the semantic load, one fixes how many neurons in total are active, making $K$ higher if more concepts are being represented. The maximum number of concepts that can be encoded in an episode is thus:
 \begin{equation}
     N^\textrm{region}_\textrm{concept} = \frac{K^\textrm{region}_\textrm{episode}}{K^\textrm{region}_\textrm{concept}}
 \end{equation}
 Therefore, we can define \textsc{Activate} (Algorithm \ref{alg:activate}) as an algorithm that maps presynaptic input $\hat{X}$, given a semantic load $S_L$, into neural activation $X$ in the following way:
\begin{algorithm}[h!]
\caption[]{Neuronal Activation}\label{alg:activate}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{Activate}{$\hat{X}$, $\textrm{region}$, $S_L$}
    \State Compute number of active neurons:
        \[
            K \gets S_L \times K^\textrm{region}_\textrm{concept}
        \]
    \State Select top-$K$ entries:
        \[
            X \gets \textrm{top-}K(\hat{X})
        \]
    \State \Return $X$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection*{Feed-forward input processing}
Input processing is computed for a single pre and post region at a time, with pre-activation input $\hat{X}^\textrm{post}$ obtained via
 \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}
    \DIFadd{\hat{X}^\textrm{post} = W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}} \cdot X^\textrm{pre} + b^\textrm{post}\odot\textrm{IM}^\textrm{post}
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
$b^\textrm{post}$ is the extra excitability that immature neurons have in region post.
\subsubsection*{Pattern completion (recurrent input processing)}
Given a recurrent network, pattern completion is typically defined as an iterative update on neural activity $X^\textrm{region}$ that is initiated in an initial state $X^\textrm{region}_0$:
\begin{equation}
    X^{\textrm{region}}_{k+1} = \textrm{top-}K(W^{\textrm{region}\leftarrow\textrm{region}}\cdot X^\textrm{region}_{k})
\end{equation}
with $W^{\textrm{region}\leftarrow\textrm{region}}$ the region recurrent connectivity matrix, $K$ dependent on the specified semantic load $S_L$, and $k$ a timestep operating on a smaller timescale,  than the overall timescale $t$. This means that within a timestep $t$, there are pattern\_complete\_iterations iterations on temporal index $k$. This is used to define \textsc{PatternComplete}  as Algorithm \ref{alg:pattern_complete}:
\begin{algorithm}[h!]
\caption[]{Pattern Completion}\label{alg:pattern_complete}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
 \DIFaddbegin \Function{PatternComplete}{$X_0$, \texttt{region}, $S_L$}
    \DIFaddend \State $X \gets X_0$
     \DIFaddbegin \For{$\text{iteration} = 1$ to $\texttt{region.pattern\_complete\_iterations}$}
        \DIFaddend \State $\hat{X} \gets W^{\textrm{region} \leftarrow \textrm{region}} \cdot X$
    \State $X \gets \textsc{Activate}\bigl(\hat{X}, \textrm{region}, S_L \bigr)$
    \EndFor
    \State \Return $X$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\subsubsection*{Hebbian Learning}
The connection between a presynaptic neuron $j$ and a postsynaptic neuron $i$ subject to Hebbian learning is updated as:
\begin{equation}
            \Delta W_{ij} = \lambda_{ij}^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}}
            X_i^\textrm{post} X_j^\textrm{pre}
\end{equation}
for a presynaptic region pre and a postsynaptic region post, where $\lambda_{ij}^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}}$ is the learning rate from neuron $j$ in region pre to neuron $i$ in region post.
\paragraph*{Maturity-dependent learning rate}
In order to combine one-shot and statistical learning (see \shortciteA{albesagonzalez_2025}), the learning rate of connections depends on the \textit{maturity} of neurons, which is given by $\textrm{IM}$. Neurons start in an \textit{immature} state $\textrm{IM}_i = 1$, allowing them to form initial \textit{connectivity  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{estimates}\DIFaddend }. Once this initial connectivity is formed, the learning rate is reduced to capture the statistical regularities of the neural activity patterns (the neuron becomes \textit{mature}, $\textrm{IM}_i = 0$). To model this, we define a quick learning rate
\begin{equation}
        \lambda^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\textrm{quick, pre/post} = \frac{W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\textrm{max-in/max-out}}{K^\textrm{pre/post}} 
        \label{eq:quick_lambda}
\end{equation}
where again $W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\textrm{max-in}$ (\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{or }\DIFaddend $W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\textrm{max-out}$)  is a network parameter determining the maximum sum of incoming (or outgoing) connections the matrix $W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}$ has for every postsynaptic (or presynaptic) neuron. Then, at each Hebbian update, we have for incoming-bounded connections:
 \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}
    \DIFadd{\lambda_{ij}^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}} = (1 - \textrm{IM}^\textrm{post}_i)\lambda^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}}_\textrm{slow} + \textrm{IM}^\textrm{post}_i\lambda^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\textrm{quick, pre}
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
and for outgoing-bounded connections:
 \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}
    \DIFadd{\lambda_{ij}^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}} = (1 - \textrm{IM}^\textrm{pre}_j)\lambda^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}}_\textrm{slow} + \textrm{IM}^\textrm{pre}_j\lambda^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\textrm{quick, post}
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
where $\lambda^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}}_\textrm{slow}$ is a network parameter indicating the slow learning rate (used throughout all simulations except for the first plasticity event in which the neuron transitions from immature to mature).
\newline\newline
Note how the quick learning rate (Eq. \eqref{eq:quick_lambda}) is defined such that when the neuron transitions from immature to mature, with the  \textit{first shot of learning}, the condition $W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\textrm{max-in}$, for incoming normalized weights, is met:
 \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}
    \DIFadd{\sum_j W_{ij} = \sum_j W_{ij}(t = 0) + \sum_j \Delta W_{ij} \approx \;\; 0 (W_{ij} \;\; \textrm{Gaussian})  +\lambda^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\textrm{quick, pre}\cdot K^\textrm{pre} = W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\textrm{max-in}
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
(and similarly for outgoing normalized weights). Also note how for incoming-bounded connections, as are feed-forward synapses, this makes the formed receptive field proportional to  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$X^\textrm{pre}$}\DIFaddend , (making it maximally selective to it). For outgoing-bounded connections, here recurrent cortical synapses, this is equivalent to making $X^\textrm{post}$ the initial guess in meaning of neuron $j$.
\paragraph*{Hebbian learning algorithm}
Altogether, this defines \textsc{Hebbian} (Algorithm \ref{alg:hebbian}) as the following update in $W^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}}$:
\begin{algorithm}
\caption[]{Hebbian Learning}\label{alg:hebbian}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
 \DIFaddbegin \Function{Hebbian}{$\textrm{post}$, $\textrm{pre}$, \texttt{sleep}}
    \DIFaddend \State Obtain learning rate matrix:
    \DIFaddbegin \If{\texttt{frozen}}
        \State \Return
    \EndIf
    \DIFaddend \If{$\textrm{post} \neq \textrm{pre}$}
         \DIFaddbegin \[
            \DIFadd{\lambda_{ij}^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}}
            \gets
            (1-\textrm{IM}^\textrm{post}_i)\,\lambda^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}}_\textrm{slow}
            + \textrm{IM}^\textrm{post}_i\,\lambda^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_{\textrm{quick, pre}}
        }\]\DIFaddend 
    \ElsIf{$\textrm{post} = \textrm{pre} = \mathrm{CTX}$ AND sleep}
         \DIFaddbegin \[
            \DIFadd{\lambda_{ij}^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}}
            \gets
            (1-\textrm{IM}^\textrm{pre}_j)\,\lambda^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}}_\textrm{slow}
            + \textrm{IM}^\textrm{pre}_j\,\lambda^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_{\textrm{quick, post}}
        }\]\DIFaddend 
    \Else
         \DIFaddbegin \[
            \DIFadd{\lambda_{ij}^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}} 
            \gets 
            \lambda^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\textrm{slow}
        }\]\DIFaddend 
    \EndIf
    \State Compute Hebbian update with activity outer product:
        \[
            \Delta W \gets \lambda^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}} \odot 
            \Bigl(X^{\textrm{post}} \otimes X^{\textrm{pre}}\Bigr)
        \]
    \State Update weights:
         \DIFaddbegin \[
            \DIFadd{W^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}} 
            \gets 
            W^{\textrm{post} \leftarrow \textrm{pre}} + \Delta W
        }\]\DIFaddend 
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\subsubsection*{Homeostatic Plasticity}
We use multiplicative normalization that guarantees, for every weight matrix $W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}$, the total sum of incoming or outgoing connections is capped to a certain value. Following \shortciteA{albesagonzalez_2025}, we use \textit{incoming homeostasis} as:
\begin{equation}
W_{ij}^{k+1}=
\begin{cases}
 W_{ij}^{k}\cdot\frac{W_\textrm{max-in}}{\sum_{j}W^{k}_{ij}} & \text{if }\sum_{j}W^{k}_{ij} = W_\textrm{max}^\textrm{in} + \epsilon_i, \;\; \epsilon_i > 0 \\[1.2ex]
W^k_{ij}& \textrm{else}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $W^k_{ij}$ denotes the value of synapse $ij$ before applying homeostasis and $W^{k+1}_{ij}$ the value after applying homeostasis (for readability, we simply write $W \equiv W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}$).
Similarly, we have \textit{outgoing homeostasis} to be defined by:
 \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}
\DIFadd{W_{ij}^{k+1} =
\begin{cases}
  W_{ij}^{k}\frac{W_\textrm{max-out}}{\sum_{i}W^k_{ij}} & \text{if }\sum_{i}W^k_{ij} = W_\textrm{max}^\textrm{out} + \epsilon_j, \;\; \epsilon_j > 0 \\[1.2ex]
W^k_{ij} & \textrm{else}
\end{cases}
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
This allows, for a presynaptic region pre and a postsynaptic region post, defining \textsc{Homeostasis} as Algorithm \ref{alg:homeostasis}.
\subsubsection*{Fixed points of synaptic dynamics}
The combination of \textit{K-winners-take-all} ($\textrm{top-}K$) activation function with Hebbian and homeostatic plasticity as defined here has been recently shown \shortcite{albesagonzalez_2025} to make synaptic connections converge to conditional firing probabilities. For outgoing homeostasis, the fixed points are:
  \begin{equation}
     W_{ij}^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}, \; \textrm{out}}(\infty) \equiv \frac{W_\textrm{max-out}^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}}{K^\textrm{post}}p(X^\textrm{post}_i = 1| X^\textrm{pre}_j = 1)
 \end{equation}
 where $K^\textrm{post}$ corresponds to the number of active neurons during plasticity. Given that the conditional probability can be at most 1, we define in this scenario $W_\textrm{max}$ as $\frac{W_\textrm{max-out}^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}}{K^\textrm{post}}$. Similarly, for incoming homeostasis, the fixed points are
    \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}
   \DIFadd{W_{ij}^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}, \; \textrm{in}}(\infty) \equiv \frac{W_\textrm{max-in}^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}}{K^\textrm{pre}}p(X^\textrm{pre}_j = 1| X^\textrm{post}_i = 1)
 }\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
 with  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$W_\textrm{max}=\frac{W_\textrm{max-in}^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}}{K^\textrm{pre}}$}\DIFaddend . These results have an important associated intuition, which is that if neurons code for individual concept (have a concept selectivity close to 1), synaptic connections reflect the associated Semantic Structure \shortcite{albesagonzalez_2025} under outgoing homeostasis (as happens in CTX). In MTL-semantic$\leftarrow$CTX and CTX$\leftarrow$MTL, which follow incoming homeostasis, when initial one-shot receptive fields induce a selectivity to a concept close to 1, feed-forward synapses slowly approximate to the associated prototype, where each synapse is becomes proportional to the contribution of each presynaptic neuron to the concept encoded in the postsynaptic neuron.
\begin{algorithm}
\caption[]{Homeostasis}\label{alg:homeostasis}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{Homeostasis}{$\textrm{post}$, $\textrm{pre}$}
\State $W \gets W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}$
    \For{each post-synaptic unit $i$}
         \DIFaddbegin \If{$\sum_j W[i,j] > W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\textrm{max-in}$}
            \DIFaddend \State Normalize:
                 \DIFaddbegin \[
                \DIFadd{W}[\DIFadd{i,:}] \DIFadd{\gets W}[\DIFadd{i,:}] 
                \DIFadd{\times
                \frac{W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\textrm{max-in}}{\sum_j W[i,j]}
                }\]\DIFaddend 
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \For{each pre-synaptic unit $j$}
         \DIFaddbegin \If{$\sum_i W[i,j] > W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\textrm{max-out}$}
            \DIFaddend \State Normalize:
                 \DIFaddbegin \[
                \DIFadd{W}[\DIFadd{:,j}] \DIFadd{\gets W}[\DIFadd{:,j}] 
                \DIFadd{\times
                \frac{W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}_\text{max-out}}{\sum_i W[i,j]}
                }\]\DIFaddend 
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\subsubsection*{Replay}
We define \textrm{Replay} as a learning mechanism involving a pre and a post region, such that $W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}$ extract statistical structure from $W^{\textrm{pre}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}$ connections. A replay operation takes as argument a specific semantic load $S_L$, which determines the conceptual complexity of the extracted regularities. To do this, we first pattern complete an initial presynaptic pattern $X^\textrm{pre}_0$, defined as a Gaussian random vector of the same size as the pre region:
\begin{equation}
     X^\textrm{pre}_{0} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_{N_\textrm{pre}})
\end{equation}
where $N_\textrm{pre}$ is the size of region pre and $\mathcal{N}(0, I_{N_\textrm{pre}})$ is a multivariate Gaussian distribution with 0 mean and identity covariance. This initial random pattern is pattern completed to obtain $X^\textrm{pre}$, and then projected to the post region, to give $X^\textrm{post}$, using in both operations the same fixed semantic load. Then, Hebbian and homeostatic plasticity operations as defined in Algorithms \ref{alg:hebbian} and \ref{alg:homeostasis} are used in the (post, pre) region pair. Replay is computed in simulations following Algorithm \ref{alg:replay}. In the text, we call replay from MTL to CTX \textit{Episodic Replay}, because it finds regularities across recurrent synapses that contain episodic information, and replay from CTX to MTL-semantic \textit{Semantic Replay}, because in this case recurrent synapses contain semantic information.
\begin{algorithm}[h!]
\caption[]{Replay}\label{alg:replay}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
 \DIFaddbegin \Function{Replay}{post, pre, $S_L^\textrm{pre}$}
    \DIFaddend \State Sample Gaussian random vector in pre:
        \[
            X_0^\textrm{pre} \gets \text{randn}(N_\textrm{pre})
        \]
    \State  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$X^\textrm{pre} \gets \textsc{PatternComplete}\bigl(X^\textrm{pre}_0, S^\textrm{pre}_L\bigr)$
    }\DIFaddend \State  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\hat{X}^\textrm{post} \gets W^{\textrm{post}\leftarrow\textrm{pre}}\cdot X^\textrm{pre} + b^\textrm{post}\odot\textrm{IM}^\textrm{post}$
}\DIFaddend \State \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$X^\textrm{post} \gets \textsc{Activate}\bigl(\hat{X}^\textrm{post},  \texttt{subregion}(S_L^\textrm{pre}), S_L=1 \bigr)$
    }\State \DIFaddend \textsc{Hebbian}(post, pre)
    \DIFaddbegin \If{post $=$ CTX}
    \If {\DIFadd{$S_L^\textrm{pre}=2$}}
\DIFaddend \State \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$X^\textrm{post} \gets \textsc{Activate}\bigl(\hat{X}^\textrm{post}, S_L=[2, 1] \bigr)$ 
}\EndIf
        \State \textsc{\DIFadd{Hebbian}}\DIFadd{(CTX, CTX, }\texttt{\DIFadd{sleep=True}}\DIFadd{)
        }\State \DIFaddend \textsc{Homeostasis}(\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{CTX, CTX)
    }\EndIf
    \State \textsc{\DIFadd{Homeostasis}}\DIFadd{(}\DIFaddend post, pre)
    \State \Return $X$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\subsection*{Learning Algorithm}
For each day of experience, assuming an input $X_\textrm{input}$ of shape $(N_\textrm{days}, T_\textrm{day}, N_\textrm{SEN})$, we define the following learning algorithm \textsc{Train} (Algorithm \ref{alg:train}), where \textrm{Wake} is defined as in Algorithm \ref{alg:wake} and the \textrm{Sleep} as in Algorithm \ref{alg:sleep}. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{When it is mentioned that the network is }\textit{\DIFadd{tested}}\DIFadd{, or }\textit{\DIFadd{frozen}}\DIFadd{, that in practice means that }\textrm{\DIFadd{Train}} \DIFadd{(Algorithm \ref{alg:train}) omits }\textrm{\DIFadd{Sleep}}\DIFadd{, with episodic memory being actually intact (but no consolidation or long-term learning takes place). }\DIFaddend All operations used have been previously defined, with these algorithms showing the order in which they are performed. \textsc{duration\_phase\_A} represents the number of days in which only episodic replay takes place (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_a}), and  \textsc{duration\_phase\_B} the number of days in which only MTL-sensory synapses are used for episodic replay, but semantic replay starts taking place (Fig. \ref{fig:phase_b}). From day \textsc{duration\_phase\_B} on, MTL replays both sensory and semantic representations (Starting with Fig. \ref{fig:phase_b_supp}). Therefore, during \textsc{duration\_phase\_A} days, episodic memories containing only sensory information are stored in MTL during Wake (Algorithm \ref{alg:wake}). During Sleep (Algorithm \ref{alg:sleep}), cortical abstractions of sensory information are formed. After \textsc{duration\_phase\_A} days, cortical abstractions are transferred to MTL-semantic via semantic replay during Sleep. Finally, after \textsc{duration\_phase\_B} days, episodic memories contain both sensory and semantic information, and so does episodic replay. This \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, together with a variable semantic load during replay, facilitates }\DIFaddend the formation of new semantic representations that are based on previous abstractions. 
\begin{algorithm}[h!]
\caption[]{Learning Algorithm Across Days}\label{alg:train}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{Train}{$X_\textrm{input}$}
    \For{$\textrm{day} = 1$ to $N_{\textrm{days}}$}
        \State Extract daily input:
            \[
            X^{\textrm{day}}_\textrm{input} \gets X_\textrm{input}[\textrm{day},:]
            \]
        \State \textsc{Wake}($X^{\textrm{day}}_\textrm{input}$)
        \State \textsc{Sleep}()
    \EndFor
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\begin{algorithm}[h!]
\caption[]{Wake}\label{alg:wake}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{Wake}{$X^{\textrm{day}}$}
        \State Reset MTL recurrent connections:
             \DIFaddbegin \[
                \DIFadd{W^{\textrm{MTL-sensory} \leftarrow \textrm{MTL-sensory}} \gets 0, W^{\textrm{MTL-semantic} \leftarrow \textrm{MTL-semantic}} \gets 0,
                W^{\textrm{MTL} \leftarrow \textrm{MTL}} \gets 0
            }\]\DIFaddend 
    \For{$t = 1$ to $T_{\textrm{day}}$}
        \State $X^{\textrm{SEN}} \gets \textsc{Activation}\bigl(X^{\textrm{day}}[t], \textrm{SEN}\bigr)$
        \State $\hat{X}^{\textrm{MTL-sensory}} 
            \gets 
            W^{\textrm{MTL-sensory} \leftarrow \textrm{SEN}} 
            \cdot X^{\textrm{SEN}}$
        \State $X^{\textrm{MTL-sensory}} 
            \gets 
            \textsc{Activation}\bigl(\hat{X}^{\textrm{MTL-sensory}}, \textrm{MTL-sensory}\bigr)$
        \State $\hat{X}^{\textrm{MTL-semantic}} 
            \gets 
            \text{randn}(N_\textrm{MTL-semantic})$
        \State $X^{\textrm{MTL-semantic}} 
            \gets 
            \textsc{Activation}\bigl(\hat{X}^{\textrm{MTL\_semantic}}, \textrm{MTL\_semantic}\bigr)$
        \State $X^{\textrm{MTL}} 
            \gets 
            [X^{\textrm{MTL-sensory}}, \; X^{\textrm{MTL-semantic}}]$
        \State  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\hat{X}^{\textrm{CTX}} 
            \gets 
            W^{\textrm{CTX} \leftarrow \textrm{MTL-sensory}}\cdot X^{\textrm{MTL-sensory}}  + b^\textrm{CTX}\odot\textrm{IM}^\textrm{CTX}$
        }\DIFaddend \State $X^{\textrm{CTX}} 
            \gets 
            \textsc{Activation}\bigl(\hat{X}^{\textrm{CTX}}, \textrm{CTX}\bigr)$
         \DIFaddbegin \If{day $>$ duration \_phase\_A}
            \DIFaddend \State  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\hat{X}^{\textrm{MTL-semantic}} 
                \gets 
                W^{\textrm{MTL-semantic} \leftarrow \textrm{CTX}}
                \cdot X^{\textrm{CTX}} +  b^\textrm{MTL-semantic}\odot\textrm{IM}^\textrm{MTL-semantic}$
            }\DIFaddend \State $X^{\textrm{MTL-semantic}} 
                \gets 
                \textsc{Activation}\bigl(\hat{X}^{\textrm{MTL-semantic}}, \textrm{MTL-semantic}\bigr)$
            \State $X^{\textrm{MTL}}[N_\textrm{MTL-sensory}:] 
                \gets 
                X^{\textrm{MTL\_semantic}}$
            \State \textsc{Hebbian}$(\textrm{MTL\_semantic}, \textrm{MTL\_semantic})$
        \EndIf
        \If{day $\ge$ duration\_phase\_B}
            \State  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\hat{X}^{\textrm{CTX}} 
                \gets 
                W^{\textrm{CTX} \leftarrow \textrm{MTL}}\cdot X^{\textrm{MTL}} +  b^\textrm{CTX}\odot\textrm{IM}^\textrm{CTX}$
            }\DIFaddend \State $X^{\textrm{CTX}} 
                \gets 
                \textsc{Activation}\bigl(\hat{X}^{\textrm{CTX}}, \textrm{CTX}\bigr)$
        \EndIf
        \State \textsc{Hebbian}$(\textrm{MTL}, \textrm{MTL})$
        \State \textsc{Hebbian}$(\textrm{MTL-sensory}, \textrm{MTL-sensory})$
        \State \textsc{Hebbian}$(\textrm{CTX}, \textrm{CTX})$
        \State \textsc{Homeostasis}$(\textrm{CTX}, \textrm{CTX})$
    \EndFor
    \State Increment day counter
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\begin{algorithm}[h!]
\caption[]{Sleep}\label{alg:sleep}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{Sleep}{}
\State Episodic Replay
    \For{$t = 1$ to $\texttt{sleep\_duration\_A}$}
        \If{day $\le$ duration\_phase\_B}
            \State \textsc{Replay}(\textrm{CTX}$, \textrm{MTL-sensory}$,  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$S_L^\textrm{pre}= 1$}\DIFaddend )
        \Else
            \State \textsc{Replay}(\textrm{CTX}$, \textrm{MTL}$,  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$S_L^\textrm{pre}= S_L[t]$}\DIFaddend )
        \EndIf
    \EndFor

     \DIFaddbegin \If{day $>$ duration\_phase\_A}
        \DIFaddend \For{$t = 1$ to $\texttt{sleep\_duration\_B}$}
            \State \textsc{Replay}(\textrm{MTL-semantic}$, \textrm{CTX}$,  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$S_L^\textrm{pre}= 1$}\DIFaddend )
        \EndFor
    \EndIf
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\DIFaddend \subsection*{Considerations regarding modeling choices}
\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The present work has required making several modeling choices, ranging from architectural decisions to model and input generation parameters. In the main results, we have already explored variations of certain critical input parameters, such as noise levels (variations across signal-to-noise ratio) and statistics (variations across episode generation probability distributions). Another relevant parameter studied was the amount of sleep (equivalent to the number of replayed episodes per sleep cycle). However, here we discuss other parameters and modeling choices that have been held at their default values in the main simulations For each, we provide a motivation and/or do additional simulations showing the model performance across parameter sweeps.
}\paragraph*{\DIFadd{Input}}
\DIFaddend \begin{itemize}
    \item  \DIFaddbegin \textbf{\DIFadd{Day length: }}\DIFadd{Here, all }\textit{\DIFadd{days}} \DIFadd{consist of 16 episodes of 5 timesteps each (80 timesteps in total). Robustness to the number of days was studied in simulations (Fig. \ref{fig:parameter_sweep}a). As expected, given that consolidation depends on uncovering structure across overlapping patterns, when the total number of presented episodes is very small the selectivity of the formed receptive fields decreases. Behavior becomes relatively consistent for $N_\textrm{episodes} = T_\textrm{day}/T_\textrm{episode} \geq 8$, where the chances of episodes sharing conceptual structure increases.
    }\DIFaddend \item  \DIFaddbegin \textbf{\DIFadd{Episode duration:}} \DIFadd{The model remains stable across all tested episode duration values (Fig. \ref{fig:parameter_sweep}b).
}\end{itemize}
\paragraph*{\DIFadd{Network model}}
\begin{itemize}
    \DIFaddend \item  \DIFaddbegin \textbf{\DIFadd{Number of pattern-complete iterations: }} 
    \DIFadd{The model shows a slight benefit to increased pattern complete iterations when its value is $\leq 3$, then stabilizes (Fig. \ref{fig:parameter_sweep}c).
    }\DIFaddend \item  \DIFaddbegin \textbf{\DIFadd{Sparsity (and number of concepts per latent factor): }} \DIFadd{Sparsity levels are imposed in the circuit model via the choice of the different $K$ values across regions and sleep phases. At the same time, it is also directly related to the number of distinct concepts that can be orthogonally encoded in a group of neurons. We therefore co-vary the network sparsity with the number of distinct concepts within latent factors $A$ and $B$, and the network size. For example, a sparsity of 0.1 is associated with the representation of 1+1 out of 10+10 distinct concepts. This in turn means that more cortical neurons are needed to represent a total of 20 simple concepts, and 100 complex concepts. No qualitative change in network selectivity after learning for different sparsity levels was observed (Fig. \ref{fig:parameter_sweep}d).
    }\DIFaddend \item  \DIFaddbegin \textbf{\textit{\DIFadd{K-winners-take-all}} \DIFadd{activation function: }}\DIFadd{Some of the most typical activation functions in neural networks include sigmoidal, step (Heaviside), or rectified linear unit (ReLU) functions. However, these tend to suffer from representational collapse when attractor dynamics deal with highly overlapping patterns such as the ones used here. A top-K activation function, which we use here, can be seen as a generalization of a step function in which the threshold is dynamically adjusted such that the number of active neurons remains stable (or meta-controlled, as in this same study we allow different sparsity levels across learning). In turn, a dynamic threshold can be interpreted as adjustable inhibition  with a biological inspiration in adjustable inhibition, which has been observed experimentally }\shortcite{wehr_2003, vogels_2011}\DIFadd{. While the particular implementation of the dynamics that maintain sparsity levels stable is abstracted, we speculate one of the functional roles of adjustable inhibition is likely to be the facilitation of learning in the presence of highly correlated overlapping patterns.
    }\DIFaddend \item  \DIFaddbegin \textbf{\DIFadd{Neurogenesis, excitability and plasticity rates: }}\DIFadd{Our dictionary learning mechanism depends on immature neurons being more excitable and plastic than mature neurons. Evidence for this has been found experimentally }\shortcite{aimone_2011}\DIFadd{, and has been used in computational models }\shortcite{gozel_2021}\DIFadd{.
    }\item \textbf{\DIFadd{Homeostatic regimes across connectivity: }}\DIFadd{In additional simulations (Fig. \ref{fig:parameter_sweep}e) we find that, in our model, incoming homeostasis (the sum of incoming synapses is capped) is necessary for selective receptive field formation in feed-forward synapses (as opposed to outgoing homeostasis). Incoming homeostasis is a crucial ingredient for our proposed mechanisms for replay-based dictionary learning, with receptive fields converging to }\textit{\DIFadd{what is the average pattern that causes active neurons to fire?}}\DIFadd{. Outgoing homeostasis reverses the map from conditional firing probabilities to connectivity, breaking the learning algorithm. Incoming normalization (akin to L1-norm regularization) is one of the most common choices in synaptic homeostasis, with recent work exploring learning in outgoing homeostatic regimes}\shortcite{fung_2023}\DIFadd{, and can be derived from spike-time-dependent synaptic plasticity }\shortcite{gutig_2003}\DIFadd{.
}\DIFaddend \end{itemize}

\subsection*{Data Analysis}
\subsubsection*{Neuronal Selectivity}
Given a  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{matrix }\DIFaddend $X$ that contains neuronal activity across T timesteps -with dimensions $(T, N_\textrm{neurons})$- \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, we define a }\textit{\DIFadd{concept indicator matrix}} \DIFaddend $Z$ \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, a binary matrix that encodes the presence (or absence) of concepts across timesteps. In practice, $Z(t)$ takes the form
}\[
\DIFadd{Z_t
=
\big[
\mathbb{I}(A(t)=A_1),\ldots,\mathbb{I}(A(t)=A_{|A|}),
\mathbb{I}(B(t)=B_1),\ldots,\mathbb{I}(B(t)=B_{|B|})
\big].
}\]
\DIFadd{with dimensions  $(T, |A| + |B|)$, when selectivity to simple concepts is examined, and 
}\[
\DIFadd{Z_t
=
\big[
\mathbb{I}(A(t)=A_1, B(t)=B_{|B|}),\ldots, \mathbb{I}(A(t)=A_{|A|}, B(t)=B_{|B|})
\big].
}\]
\DIFadd{with dimensions  $(T, |A||B|)$, when selectivity to complex concepts is examined. To measure selectivity, we }\DIFaddend obtain the normalized values:
\begin{equation}
    X_\textrm{norm} = \frac{X - \langle X \rangle_{\textrm{time}}}{\sqrt{\langle X^2 \rangle_\textrm{time} - \langle X \rangle_\textrm{time}^2}} \;\; ; \;\; Z_\textrm{norm} = \frac{Z - \langle Z \rangle_{\textrm{time}}}{\sqrt{\langle Z^2 \rangle_\textrm{time} - \langle Z \rangle_\textrm{time}^2}}
\end{equation} 
And then obtain the matrix 
\begin{equation}
    S = \frac{1}{T}X_\textrm{norm}^TZ_\textrm{norm}
\end{equation}
which, for neuron $i$ and  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{concept }\DIFaddend $j$ contains their correlation as:
 \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}
    \DIFadd{S(i, j) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t X_{\textrm{norm}, ti}Z_{\textrm{norm}, tj}
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
The Maximum Selectivity of neuron $i$ is the maximum selectivity value of neuron $i$ across all  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{factor-values (concepts) }\DIFaddend $j$:
\begin{equation}
    S_\textrm{max}(i) = \max_{j}{S(i, j)}
\end{equation}
\subsubsection*{Neuron Ordering}
 \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Given a selectivity matrix $S \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\textrm{neurons} \times N_\textrm{concepts}}$, we construct an ordering of neurons into concept-specific assemblies of fixed size $K_\textrm{assembly}$. The procedure is iterative and greedy. At each round, the concepts encoded across the second dimension are visited in a random order. For each concept in that order, we assign the still-unassigned neuron with the highest selectivity for that concept, and remove that neuron from the global pool of available neurons. This process is repeated until each concept has been assigned exactly $K_\textrm{assembly}$ neurons. The final ordered index vector is then obtained by concatenating the neurons belonging to each concept-specific assembly. This effectively returns a permutation of a subset of the given neurons, organizing them in blocks of $K_\textrm{assembly}$ units that code (as much as possible) for the same concept. Note how a natural choice is $K_\textrm{assembly} = K^\textrm{region}_\textrm{concept}$.
}\DIFaddend \subsubsection*{Signal-to-Noise Ratio}
In order to obtain the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) of a generated pattern, we first define the signal  $\mathcal{S}$  as the expected overlap (number of common neurons) of the corrupted pattern with the original, and subtract the expected overlap between two randomly generated patterns of $K^\textrm{region}$ active neurons across $N^\textrm{region}$ neurons:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}  =  \textrm{E}\Big[|| X^\textrm{corrupted} \odot X^\textrm{original}||_0\Big] - \textrm{E}\Big[|| X^\textrm{random-1} \odot X^\textrm{random-2}||_0\Big]
\end{equation}
Because we fix that $N_\textrm{swaps}$ active \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{neurons }\DIFaddend (in the  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{de-noised }\DIFaddend pattern) flip from 1 to 0, the first term is always $K^\textrm{region} - N_\textrm{swaps}$, while the second corresponds to the expected value of a hypergeometric distribution, given by $(K^\textrm{region})^2/N^\textrm{region}$. Then, we define the noise to be the number of non-common neurons between the corrupted and the original pattern, which is $2N_\textrm{swaps}$, giving:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{SNR} = \frac{\Big(K - N^\textrm{swaps}- K^2/N\Big)^2}{\Big(2N^\textrm{swaps}\Big)^2}
    \label{eq:snr}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{\DIFadd{Mutual Information}}
\paragraph*{\DIFadd{Discrete Mutual Information}}
\DIFaddend To quantify how \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{informative neural activity is about latent variables, we use discrete mutual information and entropy estimated from the empirical distribution of awake samples. For two discrete random variables $X$ and $Z$, with empirical joint distribution $p(x,z)$ and marginals $p(x)$ and $p(z)$, the mutual information is
}\[
\DIFadd{I(X;Z)
=
\sum_{x,z} p(x,z)\log_2\!\left(\frac{p(x,z)}{p(x)p(z)}\right),
}\]
\DIFadd{and the entropy of $Z$ is
}\[
\DIFadd{H(Z)
=
-\sum_z p(z)\log_2 p(z).
}\]
\paragraph*{\DIFadd{Normalized Mutual Information (NMI)}}
\DIFadd{When reporting normalized mutual information (NMI), we divide by the entropy of the latent variable,
}\[
\DIFadd{\mathrm{NMI}(X;Z)
=
\frac{I(X;Z)}{H(Z)},
}\]
\DIFadd{with the convention that $\mathrm{NMI}=0$ if $H(Z)=0$. This normalization yields values between $0$ and $1$, where $0$ indicates that neural activity carries no information about the latent variable and $1$ indicates that it fully determines it.
}\paragraph{\DIFadd{Simple Concepts NMI and Complex Concepts NMI}}
\DIFadd{The latent variable $Z$ is always binary and indicates whether a particular concept is present at a given timestep. For }\textit{\DIFadd{simple concepts}} \DIFadd{normalized mutual information, we define one binary variable for each simple concept $A_i$ and $B_j$:
}\[
\DIFadd{Z_t^{A_i} = \mathbf{1}[A_i \in e_t],
\qquad
Z_t^{B_j} = \mathbf{1}[B_j \in e_t].
}\]
\DIFadd{For }\textit{\DIFadd{complex}} \DIFadd{normalized mutual information, we instead define one binary variable for each episode-level conjunction $A_iB_j$:
}\[
\DIFadd{Z_t^{A_iB_j} = \mathbf{1}[e_t = (A_i,B_j)].
}\]
\DIFadd{Thus, the distinction between simple concepts and complex concepts NMI lies entirely in which latent variables are used: simple concepts correspond to latent factor values (e.g. $A_3$), whereas complex concepts correspond to their conjunction (e.g. $A_3B_2$).
}\newline\newline
\paragraph{\DIFadd{NMI Learning Trajectories}}
\DIFadd{To construct the mutual-information learning trajectories (Figs. \ref{fig:phase_a} \ref{fig:higher-order}), we compute NMI in sliding windows over time. For each latent variable $Z^\ell$, each neuron activity trace $X_n$ is restricted to a time window, and we compute
}\[
\DIFadd{\mathrm{NMI}_{n,\ell}(w)
=
\frac{I(X_n^{(w)}; Z_\ell^{(w)})}{H(Z_\ell)},
}\]
\DIFadd{where $(w)$ denotes the samples contained in that window, and \(H(Z_\ell)\)is estimated over the full recording used for that trajectory. Neurons are first grouped into concept-specific assemblies using the selectivity-based ordering described above, and the reported trajectory for latent $\ell$ is obtained by averaging $\mathrm{NMI}_{n,\ell}(w)$ across the neurons assigned to the corresponding assembly. In this way, the trajectories quantify how strongly each learned neural assembly tracks its associated latent variable (concept) as training progresses. Each window \(w\) contains \(T_\textrm{NMI}\) consecutive timesteps, and successive windows are offset by a stride of \(\Delta T_\textrm{NMI}\) timesteps, which is a way of estimating the evolution of mutual information across time, assuming it remains locally stable across small temporal windows.
}\subsubsection*{\DIFadd{Probability of adding a concept to the cortical vocabulary (forming a receptive field in cortex that is selective to a particular concept)}}
\DIFadd{To quantify how }\DIFaddend exposure statistics shape cortical dictionary formation (Figs.  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\ref{fig:phase_a}g }\DIFaddend and \ref{fig:higher-order}f), we measure, across many independently simulated networks, whether a targeted simple concept or a targeted  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{abstract episode (complex concept) }\DIFaddend becomes part of the cortical dictionary after a \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{single }\DIFaddend session of sleep as a function of its probability of occurrence in the training distribution. In both analyses, each simulation produces a binary formation outcome for  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{focal items}\DIFaddend , and the plotted quantity is the empirical probability of formation obtained by averaging this binary outcome across all simulations sharing the same focal probability.
 \DIFaddbegin \newline\newline
\DIFaddend For the simple-concept analysis,  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{each simulation defines independent marginal distributions over the two latent factors $A$ and $B$}\DIFaddend . In each  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{dimension separately, one focal concept is chosen uniformly at random}\DIFaddend , and its marginal probability is  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{sampled from the }\DIFaddend grid
\[
\{0.1,0.2,\dots,0.9\}.
\]
 \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{If the focal concept in dimension $A$ is $A_{i^\ast}$, we set
}\[
\DIFadd{p(A_{i^\ast}) = p^\ast_A,
}\]
\DIFadd{and distribute the remaining mass $1-p^\ast_A$ across the other four $A$ concepts using a Dirichlet draw. The same construction is performed independently for dimension $B$, yielding
}\[
\DIFadd{p(B_{j^\ast}) = p^\ast_B,
}\]
\DIFadd{with the remaining mass $1-p^\ast_B$ }\DIFaddend distributed across the other  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$B$ concepts by an independent Dirichlet draw. Episode }\DIFaddend probabilities are then constructed as  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{a product distribution,
}\[
\DIFadd{p(A_i,B_j)=p(A_i)\,p(B_j).
}\]\DIFaddend 
 \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Thus, increasing the focal probability of a concept affects all episodes containing that concept proportionally, while concepts belonging to the same latent factor compete for the remaining marginal mass. By contrast, }\DIFaddend the  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{opposite latent factor is unaffected except through the multiplicative factor implied by the independent product structure.
}\newline\newline
\DIFaddend For the complex concepts, we  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{evaluate the first }\DIFaddend day of training with $S_L^\textrm{max}=2$  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(allowing semantic load 2 during replay)}\DIFaddend . In each simulation, one focal episode $(A_i,B_j)$ is chosen uniformly from the $25$ possible episodes, and its probability of occurrence in training is sampled from a discrete grid
\[
\{0,0.025,0.05,\dots,0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8,0.9,1.0\}.
\]
The focal episode is assigned this probability, and the remaining mass is distributed  across the other $24$ episodes  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{again with Dirichlet sampling.
}\newline\newline
\DIFaddend After training, we evaluate whether the focal  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{concept (simple or complex) }\DIFaddend has been incorporated into the  cortical dictionary. To do so, we extract the receptive fields from  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{CTX, }\DIFaddend and compare them with  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{de-noised representations of each concept in MTL-sensory}\DIFaddend . For each  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{concept, we obtain
}\begin{equation}
\DIFadd{s_c=\max_n \cos\!\big(\mathbf{p}_c,\mathbf{w}_n\big),
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
where  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathbf{p}_c$ is the de-noised sensory version of concept $c$ }\DIFaddend and $\mathbf{w}_n$ is the receptive field of CTX neuron $n$. The focal  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{concept }\DIFaddend is considered \emph{added to CTX} when  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{for some receptive field the corresponding de-noised version was the one with highest cosine similarity. Each }\DIFaddend simulation yields a pair
 \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}
\DIFadd{\big( p_{\mathrm{focal}}, \mathbb{I}(\textrm{focal added})\big),
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
and the plotted curve is obtained by averaging  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathbb{I}(\textrm{focal added})$ }\DIFaddend across all simulations with the same focal episode probability.

In the test partition, we average activity within each selected neuronal group and decode episode identity from the most active group (Fig. \ref{fig:normative}d)}\DIFaddend .

For samples of }\DIFaddend MTL-sensory  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{replayed (with semantic load 2), we find its closest noiseless pattern (which de-noised sensory pattern would be more similar to the replayed one?) and infer the effective corruption level of replay by computing the Hamming distance between the evaluated replay pattern and its closest prototype, converting this distance to the equivalent number of input swaps, and then computing the corresponding SNR.
}\DIFaddend \newline\DIFaddbegin \newline
\DIFadd{We then estimate the }\textit{\DIFadd{Replayed/Awake Generalization}} \DIFadd{of the replayed patterns (Fig. \ref{fig:normative}f) Intuitively, we want to answer: }\textit{\DIFadd{how good are the replayed patterns at identifying during wakefulness the concept they are more selective to?}} \DIFadd{For each network, for each complex concept $A_iB_j$, we take 10 random replayed MTL-sensory patterns which, interpreted as receptive fields, that is their preferred concept. Then, we take the inputs used to test Compositional Generalization and, for every day, we create an episode classifier with one random receptive field from the reservoir for each concept. Then, we use that classifier (class established from the complex concept whose cosine similarity with each input pattern is highest) and obtain an accuracy measure. The average over days (and thus over random classifiers built from replayed MTL-sensory patterns) is the final Replayed / Awake Generalization metric.

\subsubsection*{Concept cell formation \shortcite{quiroga_2005, quiroga_2012}}
\paragraph{Experimental goal.}
We  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{tested }\DIFaddend concept-cell formation via different measures aimed at quantifying their main defining properties: a high selectivity to individual concepts that generalizes across contexts and metrics. To do so, we measure (i) cross-context linear decodability, (ii) concept selectivity and (iii) normalized mutual information.

\paragraph{Network }
We use a pre-trained network (same training regime as Figure  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\ref{fig:phase_b}}\DIFaddend ) and evaluate activity in MTL-sensory and MTL-semantic.


\paragraph{Cross-context linear decodability of concept representations}
To quantify whether individual concepts are represented in a way that generalizes across episodic contexts , we use a cross-context linear decoding assay. We  evaluate activity in MTL-sensory and MTL-semantic under frozen weights  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{across sensory-corruption levels $N_{\textrm{swaps}}\in\{1,2,\ldots,15\}$.
}\DIFaddend \newline\newline
For each target concept  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$c\in A\cup B$}\DIFaddend , we construct a binary classification task. If the target is $A_i$ ( \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$c=A_i$}\DIFaddend ), we  sample one concept $B_j$ to define a \textit{training context}; if the target is $B_j$, we analogously sample one concept $A_i$. Training data  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{consist of $N_{\textrm{blocks}}^{\textrm{train}}=2$ deterministic blocks}\DIFaddend : one positive episode containing the target concept  and one negative episode in which the target concept is replaced by a contrast concept from the same  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{latent factor}\DIFaddend . Thus, for a target $A_i$ and training context $B_j$, the two training episodes are $(A_i,B_j)$ and $(A_k,B_j)$ \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, with $k\neq i$}\DIFaddend . Likewise, for a target $B_j$ and training context $A_i$, the two training episodes are $(A_i,B_j)$ and $(A_i,B_\ell)$ \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, with $\ell\neq j$. Each block lasts $T_{\textrm{episode}}^{\textrm{train}}=1$ timestep}\DIFaddend , yielding a minimal balanced  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{decoder-training }\DIFaddend set by construction.
 \DIFaddbegin 

\DIFaddend From this deterministic  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{input sequence}\DIFaddend , we extract awake activity from MTL-sensory and MTL-semantic, denoted $X^\textrm{MTL-sensory}$ and $X^\textrm{MTL-semantic}$. Labels are defined as
 \DIFaddbegin \[
\DIFadd{Y_c =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if concept } c \text{ is present in the episode},\\
0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
}\]\DIFaddend 
For each region separately, we  train a linear logistic classifier to predict $Y_c$ from the corresponding neural activity. In all cases, the same labels are used for both regions, so differences in decoding performance reflect only differences in the underlying neural representations.
\newline\newline
To test cross-context generalization, we generate  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{an excluded-context test dataset lasting $T_{\textrm{day}}^{\textrm{test}}=200$ timesteps, with episodes of duration $T_{\textrm{episode}}^{\textrm{test}}=5$ timesteps. If the decoder is }\DIFaddend trained to recognize $A_i$ in the context of $B_j$,  all test episodes containing $B_j$ are  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{excluded}\DIFaddend . The remaining probability mass is redistributed so that the marginal probability of the target concept remains  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$0.5$, whereas the remaining $0.5$ }\DIFaddend is distributed uniformly across non-target concepts. The same logic is applied symmetrically when decoding a target $B_j$.  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Thus, }\DIFaddend the target concept can only be  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{decoded }\DIFaddend if its representation generalizes beyond the specific opposite-attribute context used  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{for decoder }\DIFaddend training.
\newline\newline
For each target concept, we repeat this procedure  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{for $N_{\textrm{repeats}}=10$ }\DIFaddend random choices of training context and contrast concept. Decoder performance is quantified as held-out classification accuracy on the excluded-context test  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{dataset. For each value of $N_{\textrm{swaps}}$, the }\DIFaddend final cross-context linear decodability score for each region is obtained by  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{averaging accuracy }\DIFaddend across repeats and across all target concepts in  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$A\cup B$. Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}b1 compares }\DIFaddend the resulting concept-level  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{decoding accuracies }\DIFaddend for MTL-sensory and MTL-semantic.

\subsubsection*{Consolidation promotes the emergence of representational overlap in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex \shortcite{tompary_2017}}

\paragraph{Experimental goal.}
We test whether offline consolidation increases the similarity of neural representations for episodes that share a common scene context. As in \shortciteA{tompary_2017}, overlap is evaluated before and after sleep-induced consolidation.

\paragraph{Episode set and latent structure.}
Each episode contains one object latent  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{factor ($A$}\DIFaddend ) and one scene latent  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{factor ($B$}\DIFaddend ). We use 128 distinct objects and 4 scenes. Objects are assigned evenly to scenes (32 objects per scene), yielding a deterministic training sequence in which each object is presented with its assigned scene.

\paragraph{Encoding protocol.}
Networks are initialized from scratch and trained for one wake day. Each object--scene episode is presented once, with fixed duration (5 timesteps per episode). Trial-level variability is introduced  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{again via $N_\textrm{swaps}$ bit flipping}\DIFaddend . This encoding regime produces  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$T_\textrm{episode}$ noisy realizations per episode}\DIFaddend .

\paragraph{Pre- and post-consolidation probing.}
Representational overlap is probed with partial object cues: \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{noisy ($N_\textrm{swaps} = 4$) }\DIFaddend object information is provided, whereas scene input channels are set to zero. For each cue, MTL cue is pattern completed,  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{maintaining the cued neural activity fixed. The recovered }\DIFaddend MTL state is then propagated  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{to CTX, in turn fed-back back into MTL-semantic. This done }\DIFaddend immediately before sleep (\textit{pre}) and repeated after consolidation (\textit{post}).

\paragraph{Consolidation manipulation.}
Consolidation consists of one sleep cycle including both replay streams (episodic and semantic replay). Semantic replay is enabled from the beginning of training (i.e., no initial phase with episodic-only replay). Replay durations follow the Figure experiments configuration listed in Table~\ref{tab:overlap_reproduction_parameters}.

\paragraph{Overlap metric and summary statistics.}
For each region (MTL, CTX), we compute a pairwise Pearson correlation matrix across all object-evoked probe patterns. For object $i$, we define overlap as:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{OverlapIndex}_i
=
\overline{r}_{i,\mathrm{same\ scene}}
-
\overline{r}_{i,\mathrm{different\ scene}},
\end{equation}
where $\overline{r}_{i,\mathrm{same\ scene}}$ is the mean correlation between object $i$ and other objects assigned to the same scene, and $\overline{r}_{i,\mathrm{different\ scene}}$ is the corresponding mean across objects assigned to different scenes. Positive values indicate stronger scene-dependent  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{representational overlap.

\subsubsection*{Neocortical synaptic engrams for remote contextual memories \shortcite{lee_2023}}

\paragraph{Experimental goal and circuit mapping.}
To reproduce  synaptic-engram  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{signatures }\DIFaddend of contextual fear conditioning  \DIFaddbegin \shortcite{lee_2023}\DIFadd{, we map prefrontal cortex (PFC) and basolateral amygdala (BLA) populations to CTX and }\DIFaddend MTL-semantic , respectively. We  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{use two latent factors, context $A$ and valence $B$, each comprising five concepts}\DIFaddend . We interpret  $A_1$  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{as the }\DIFaddend conditioned context (CS)  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{and $B_1$ as the fear-related unconditioned stimulus (US).
}\newline\newline
\paragraph{\DIFadd{Pre-conditioning and conditioning.}}
\DIFadd{The experiment begins with one pre-conditioning day of $T_{\textrm{pre}}$ timesteps. During this phase, $B_1$ is present in every episode, whereas the activity identities in the $A$ sensory channel are independently permuted at each timestep. The network subsequently undergoes its usual sleep consolidation cycle. The intent of this is to mimic the pre-existing fear-selective circuits in the animal. Similarly, this allows us to distinguish cortical }\textit{\DIFadd{fear}} \DIFadd{cells, as those that become tuned to the Unconditioned Stimulus, for later behavioral probing.
}\newline\newline
\DIFaddend Conditioning consists of one  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{further day of $T_{\textrm{cond}}$ timesteps. Out of these, $T_{\textrm{cond-}A_1}$ contain only the conditioned stimulus (as happens experimentally), with MTL-sensory cells corresponding to interoceptive signals being random. Then, $T_{\textrm{cond-}A_1B_1}$ further steps contain the joint presentation, which would correspond to the electric shock. Conditioning is followed by the standard sleep cycle, with semantic replay available from the beginning of the experiment and a maximum replay semantic load of $S_L^{\max}=2$.
}\DIFaddend \paragraph{Engram and recall-only  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{populations}\DIFaddend .}
 \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{We identify engram cells from their mean awake activity during the complete conditioning window. For each region, we compute the temporal mean and apply the region's standard activation function. The resulting active CTX cells define the PFC engram $\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{CTX}}$, while active }\DIFaddend MTL-semantic  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{cells define the BLA engram $\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{MTL-sem}}$.
}\newline\newline
\DIFadd{Recall consists of a further presentation of the same conditioning input, without a subsequent sleep cycle. Normal awake dynamics and plasticity remain active during this pass. Applying the same mean-activity procedure to the recall window yields $\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{CTX}}^{\textrm{recall}}$, from which we define recall-only cells $\mathcal{E}_{\textrm{CTX}}^{\textrm{recall-only}}$ the recall neurons that are not engram neurons.
}\newline\newline
\DIFaddend \paragraph{Extinction and  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{fear-expression }\DIFaddend proxy.}
Extinction  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{consists of $N_{\textrm{days}}^{\textrm{ext}}$ wake--sleep cycles. During extinction, the }\DIFaddend fear-related  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{US feature $B_1$ is absent: each of the remaining $20$ latent pairs $A_iB_j$, with $j\neq1$, is sampled with probability $1/20$. Each extinction day contains $T_{\textrm{ext}}$ timesteps, is composed of fixed-duration episodes of $T_{\textrm{episode}}^{\textrm{ext}}$ timesteps, and is followed by the standard sleep consolidation cycle.
}\newline\newline
\DIFadd{Before extinction}\DIFaddend ,  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{we identify cortical assemblies selective for the conditioned context $A_1$ and for the fear-related feature $B_1$ using a frozen probe that samples all $25$ latent pairs uniformly. After each extinction day and its subsequent sleep cycle, we }\DIFaddend quantify  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{fear-related cortical coupling as the mean recurrent CTX weight from the $A_1$-selective assembly to the $B_1$-selective assembly:
}\[
\DIFadd{F_d
=
\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{E}_{A_1}\right|
 \left|\mathcal{E}_{B_1}\right|}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{E}_{B_1}}
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{E}_{A_1}}
W^{\mathrm{CTX}\leftarrow\mathrm{CTX}}_{ij}(d).
}\]
\DIFadd{Here, rows $i$ correspond to postsynaptic CS-selective neurons and columns $j$ to presynaptic US-selective neurons. This measure tracks how extinction reshapes the predictability of fear given the conditioned stimulus. Note how these changes in semantic memory connections effectively translate to }\textit{\DIFadd{the meaning of the conditioned stimulus is less and less related to that of fear}}\DIFadd{.
}\paragraph{\DIFadd{Reconditioning and cortical attractor stability.}}
\DIFadd{To assess reconditioning after extinction}\DIFaddend , we compare  the  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{post-extinction network with the pre-conditioning checkpoint. For each random seed, independent copies of both networks receive the same $A_1B_1$ input for $T_{\textrm{recond}}$ timesteps. Awake learning remains enabled during this re-exposure, but no subsequent sleep cycle is performed. Thus, the analysis tests the stability of the cortical representation formed after a matched, sleep-free reconditioning period without modifying the stored network checkpoints. For each network state $s$, we define the reconditioned cortical engram as before (CTX activation of mean awake CTX activity). We then measure the memory stability by comparing the cortical pattern completion outcome of each network's engram with the original state (Fig. \ref{fig:validation_circuit}k). Higher values indicate that the cortical pattern recruited during reconditioning is more stable under that network's own recurrent dynamics.

To model anterograde amnesia (Fig. \ref{fig:behavioural} \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{e}\DIFaddend ), we start from a pretrained network  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{that has never seen pairs of the form }\DIFaddend $(A_i,B_i)$ . 
\newline\newline
Before learning the new pair, the cortical episode subregion corresponding to previously absent diagonal pairs is reset to an immature state, so that the new association requires fresh cortical recruitment. Two copies of the network are then exposed to the same single-day $(A_i,B_i)$ association. In the sham condition, the network is allowed to undergo normal sleep-dependent consolidation. In the anterograde condition, MTL recurrent consolidation machinery is lesioned immediately after the awake association day, by setting recurrent MTL connectivity to zero ($W^{\mathrm{MTL}\leftarrow \mathrm{MTL}}$, $W^{\mathrm{MTL\text{-}sensory}\leftarrow \mathrm{MTL\text{-}sensory}}$, and $W^{\mathrm{MTL\text{-}semantic}\leftarrow \mathrm{MTL\text{-}semantic}}$) .
\newline\newline

\DIFaddend \subsection*{Retrograde lesion of MTL for semantic recall}
To model retrograde semantic amnesia (Fig. \ref{fig:behavioural}g), we start from a pretrained network with  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{default input parameters.
}\DIFaddend \newline\newline
After this post-learning sleep phase, the retrograde condition is generated by lesioning the semantic pathway while leaving the rest of the network unchanged. Concretely, we remove  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the CTX-MTL-semantic-CTX loop.
}\DIFaddend \newline\newline
 \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Then, we test semantic recall ($B_i$ from matching pair }\DIFaddend $A_i$ \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{) turning on cortical $A_i$ assembly, which is corrupted before pattern completion (}\DIFaddend $N_\mathrm{swaps}^{\mathrm{CTX}}$ \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{). The sham network first does one full feed-forward cycle from CTX to }\DIFaddend MTL-semantic and  \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{back to CTX, then both networks undergo cortical pattern completion. Recall }\DIFaddend performance is quantified as the mean activity of the target $B_i$ cortical assembly after the $A_i$ cue.
\newline\newline
We repeat the analysis over a noise sweep with $N_\mathrm{swaps}^{\mathrm{CTX}}\in\{10,\dots,39\}$, using 10 seeds per noise level. This analysis isolates the contribution of semantic feedback to the recall of previously learned associations under degraded cortical cues.
